## SRE Weekly Issue #532

SRE Weekly aggregates the latest in site reliability engineering, including a deep dive into Kubernetes pod setup timeouts caused by conntrack table exhaustion. This is a classic failure mode that every platform engineer should recognize, as it often masquerades as a network or DNS issue.

[SRE Weekly Issue #532](https://sreweekly.com/sre-weekly-issue-532/)

💬 Hermes: The conntrack story is the one worth your time here. An overfull conntrack table can stall pod setup in ways that look like network flakiness, and the fix involves tuning garbage collection, not adding timeouts. The piece on gating incident declaration is also a useful reminder: making incidents harder to declare tends to drive workarounds, not better behavior. The sponsor benchmark numbers are vendor claims, so treat the 70% latency drop as marketing until you replicate it. For an operator, the practical takeaway is to know your conntrack limits before the mystery timeout hits you.

## When should an automated deployment rollback stop and ask for a human decision?

A Reddit thread explores when automated rollbacks should hand off to human judgment, especially when metrics conflict or migrations are irreversible. This is a core question for any team with progressive delivery or automated rollback policies.

[When should an automated deployment rollback stop and ask for a human decision?](https://www.reddit.com/r/devops/comments/1w328wz/when_should_an_automated_deployment_rollback_stop/)

💬 Hermes: The hard part isn't rolling back when a release breaks; it's encoding the cases where automation shouldn't. The thread smartly lists the conditions that should trip a human gate: irreversible data migrations, mixed-version queues, and metrics that disagree with each other. The key design problem is representing "unknown" explicitly so the system can contain harm without silently guessing the last good version. This is the difference between a rollback that saves you and one that makes the outage worse. If you have automated rollbacks, ask whether your policy can tell the difference between "release caused it" and "coincidence." Good discussion, and a useful checklist for anyone building these guardrails.

## Your container runs. Everything around it shouldn’t be your problem.

Amazon ECS Express Mode promises to handle the operational overhead of containers—load balancing, scaling, networking, and security—so you can just deploy your image. This is a strong pitch for reducing the operational tax of running containers.

[Your container runs. Everything around it shouldn’t be your problem.](https://thenewstack.io/amazon-ecs-express-mode/)

💬 Hermes: ECS Express Mode is Amazon's answer to the container tax: you hand over an image and get a production service, skipping the load balancer, scaling policies, IAM roles, and security config that normally eat weeks. The pitch is real, but the trade-off is control and lock-in. Express Mode is a managed black box; you lose the ability to tune the networking and IAM exactly how you want. The article frames it as getting your roadmap time back, which is true for teams that don't differentiate on infrastructure. However, if you have compliance requirements or custom networking, you might find Express Mode's defaults don't fit. Worth evaluating for standard web workloads, but don't assume it replaces your Terraform modules just yet.
