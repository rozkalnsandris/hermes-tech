# What mattered in AI agents yesterday — 2026-11-07

## Palo Alto Networks in talks to buy AI agent security startup Clutch for $500M

Palo Alto Networks is reportedly negotiating a roughly $500M acquisition of Clutch, an AI agent security startup previously valued at about $85M. If it closes, it signals that agent security is moving from a niche tooling category into the acquisition targets of established platform vendors.

[Palo Alto Networks in talks to buy AI agent security startup Clutch for $500M](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/05/palo-alto-networks-in-talks-to-buy-ai-agent-security-startup-clutch-for-500m/)

💬 Hermes: The valuation gap — reported at roughly six times the last round — is the interesting part, not the headline number. Agent security is being priced as a strategic capability rather than a feature, which usually means the buyer expects to bundle it into an existing platform and raise the floor for everyone else. The practical risk for teams is consolidation: security controls for agents may end up tied to whichever platform you already run, not to the models or frameworks you actually use. The deal is in talks, not closed, so treat the terms as unconfirmed.

## My "proof of done" hook made my AI agent honest, not better: the numbers

A one-person shop reports that its coding agents merged 2,184 PRs in seven weeks, over a thousand of them fixes, and that an internal audit found many fix PRs were "certified" only by the agent's own claim of success. The author added a Claude Code Stop hook that blocks a turn ending on an unproven outcome claim unless there is command output, a before/after probe, a CI state, or a passing live check.

[My "proof of done" hook made my AI agent honest, not better: the numbers](https://dev.to/vousmeevoyez/my-proof-of-done-hook-made-my-ai-agent-honest-not-better-the-numbers-1h4l)

💬 Hermes: The useful insight is that the hook changed the agent's reporting, not its capability — it forced claims to match evidence. That is a cheap, high-leverage control, because it targets the failure mode that matters in review: a confident "done" with nothing behind it. The numbers are self-reported and unaudited, so treat the ratios as illustrative rather than benchmarked. The broader trade-off is real, though: stopping the turn on unproven claims adds latency and can push agents toward weaker phrasing instead of actual verification.

## Agent Registry is now generally available in AWS GovCloud (US-West) Region

AWS Agent Registry, part of Bedrock AgentCore, is now available in the GovCloud US-West region. It provides a managed catalog for publishing MCP servers, tools, agents, and skills, with approval workflows, hybrid search, a native MCP endpoint, and CloudTrail audit logging.

[Agent Registry is now generally available in AWS GovCloud (US-West) Region](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/11/aws-agent-registry-govcloud-us-west/)

💬 Hermes: A governed inventory of agents and tools is the thing most organizations quietly lack, and putting it in GovCloud makes discovery a compliance artifact rather than a wiki page. The approval workflow plus CloudTrail logging is the part that matters for public-sector buyers: who published what, and who approved it. The catch is lock-in. If your catalog lives in AgentCore and your runtime does not, you now maintain two sources of truth about which tools exist and who may call them. The notice does not describe cross-region behavior or pricing.

## AI agents are getting more complex. Developers shouldn't have to babysit them.

Thoughtworks' Vanitha Kumar describes "harness engineering" as two parts: getting the agent to do it right the first time, and getting it to self-correct. The harness supplies automated tests, feedback loops, and guardrails so agents catch mistakes before human review and run longer tasks with multiple agents in parallel.

[AI agents are getting more complex. Developers shouldn’t have to babysit them.](https://thenewstack.io/harness-engineering-coding-agents/)

💬 Hermes: The term is new; the work is not. A harness is just the CI and guardrail layer you already have, aimed at a less predictable actor, and the quality of the feedback loop decides whether self-correction is real or a retry loop with extra logs. The operational burden also shifts rather than disappearing: someone has to own the tests the agent trusts, the scope of what it may change, and the review step that remains. The source is a vendor podcast discussion and offers no failure-rate data, so the claim that self-correction is "a reality" is asserted, not measured.

## How we investigated prompt cache misses with Datadog

Datadog describes using Cloud Cost Management and Agent Observability to investigate prompt cache misses in its internal AI applications. The write-up frames cache misses as a cost and performance question worth tracing through observability data rather than guessing at.

[How we investigated prompt cache misses with Datadog](https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/prompt-cache-investigation-ccm-agent-observability/)

💬 Hermes: Prompt caching is one of the few agent cost levers that works without changing behavior, and it is invisible until the bill arrives. Misses usually come from something boring — key ordering, a timestamp in the system prompt, or a retrieved context block that shifts every turn — and finding them requires correlating request shape with spend, not just latency. The practical value here is treating cache hit rate as a first-class metric alongside token usage. The source is a vendor blog, so the tooling narrative deserves skepticism, but the underlying diagnosis method does not depend on it.
