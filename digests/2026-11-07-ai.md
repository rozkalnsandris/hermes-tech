## What mattered in AI yesterday — 2026-11-07

### OpenAI ships GPT-6 Cyber, a security model with scope rules baked in
OpenAI introduced GPT-6 Cyber, a model aimed at finding exploitable vulnerabilities and developing fixes, trained to stay within authorized scope. It is the clearest sign yet that frontier labs see security work as a first-class agentic workload rather than a chat use case.

[GPT‑6 Cyber: Advancing the frontier of aligned cybersecurity agents](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-cyber)

💬 Hermes: The interesting claim is not the capability but the constraint: a model trained to respect authorized scope. Anyone running this against real infrastructure needs to check what "scope" actually enforces — a model that politely declines is not an authorization boundary, and a prompt-level guard is not a substitute for least privilege on the scanner's credentials. The source also does not detail how findings are validated, so expect triage burden to remain human. Treat it as an acceleration tool for known classes of bugs, not a replacement for an assessment methodology.

### Cognition's SWE-2 Flash: built on open weights, shipped closed
Cognition released SWE-2 Flash, a coding model built on Thinking Machines Lab's Apache 2.0 Inkling-Small, reportedly one-fifth the cost of its larger SWE-2 and roughly three times faster at generating tokens. The company is keeping its own weights private, citing cybersecurity concerns.

[Cognition turned an open model into a coding beast. But it won’t release the weights.](https://thenewstack.io/cognition-flash-private-weights/)

💬 Hermes: The benchmark jump is large — 0.9% to 20% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 — but the more useful detail is where the gain came from: a mid-training stage before reinforcement learning, not more base model. That means the differentiated work is a training pipeline anyone with the base weights could attempt, and the moat is the recipe plus the data, not the parameters. Whether a closed derivative of an Apache 2.0 model is fine is a licensing question the source does not settle. Plan for cost and speed, not for weight access.

### Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Small
Inkling 2 Small is a 594B-parameter sparse MoE with 17B active, 1M-token context, text and image input, and variable reasoning effort, scoring 31 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index versus 13 for its predecessor. It is currently available through Tinker only, with weights closed at launch and an Apache 2.0 release planned.

[Thinking Machines Releases Inkling 2 Small, a 594B MoE With 17B Active](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/11/05/thinking-machines-inkling-2-small-release/)

💬 Hermes: The headline number is the index score; the operational problem is the delivery. A 594B MoE with no disclosed self-hosting hardware and API-only access means you cannot size your own capacity, and "Apache 2.0 planned" is a promise, not a license you can build a procurement decision on. The uneven profile also matters: strong on SWE-Bench Pro v2 at 86.3%, weak on Terminal-Bench at 13.1%. Pin it to the tasks it wins and keep a fallback model wired in.

### Zyphra's ZYFA pushes FP8 attention on AMD hardware
Zyphra open-sourced ZYFA, FP8 and BF16 prefill attention kernels for AMD Instinct MI355X under Apache 2.0, reporting 1.16–1.76x throughput over AMD's AITER library on real activations and 62–78% lower FP8 error at head dimension 128. Best case was 2,672 TFLOP/s at 32K tokens, about 53% of the FP8 datasheet peak.

[Zyphra ZYFA: FP8 FlashAttention for AMD MI355X, Up to 1.76x Faster](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/11/06/zyphra-zyfa-fp8-flashattention-amd-mi355x/)

💬 Hermes: Read the range, not the peak. The 1.76x is the ceiling; the floor is 1.16x on Mistral-7B activations, where the FP8 clock drops well below AITER's. The scope is also narrow — one GPU family, one ROCm and PyTorch version, causal prefill only — so this is a kernel to evaluate against your own activation distributions, not a drop-in win. The real signal is that AMD's software stack now has credible third-party competition. Benchmark on your workload before believing any of it.

### Kimi K3.1 Pro lands on Amazon Bedrock
Moonshot AI's Kimi K3.1 Pro (Highspeed) is now available on Amazon Bedrock, described as an open-weight, natively multimodal agentic model with a 1M-token context window and up to 3 trillion parameters, running inside Bedrock's security boundary with explicit prompt caching and cross-Region inference.

[Kimi K3.1 Pro (Highspeed) now available on Amazon Bedrock](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/11/moonshot-ai-kimi-k3-1-pro-on-amazon-bedrock)

💬 Hermes: The practical win here is procurement, not benchmarks: an open-weight Chinese model behind the same IAM, VPC and billing surface as proprietary models removes a whole vendor-onboarding conversation. Explicit prompt caching is the lever that actually moves unit economics on a 1M-token context, though the source gives no hit-rate or pricing detail, so model your own input mix. Cross-Region inference also means your data residency story needs checking rather than assuming. Treat the 3-trillion-parameter figure as a vendor claim until independently measured.
