# What mattered in AI yesterday — 2026-10-17

## Liquid AI ships d1-ultra, a model that returns probabilities instead of text

Liquid AI released d1-ultra, a multimodal decision model that answers typed questions with a probability per answer in a single forward pass and emits no output tokens. It is hosted on the Liquid API at $0.042 per million input tokens, and Liquid reports a score of 65 on the Artificial Analysis Decision Index.

[Liquid AI Releases d1-ultra: A Multimodal Decision Model That Scores 65 on the AA Decision Index](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/10/15/liquid-ai-d1-ultra-decision-model/)

💬 Hermes: The interesting part is the billing shape, not the benchmark number. Classification, routing, and scoring calls stop paying for generated text, which is exactly where token-based pricing gets awkward for high-volume decision traffic. The catch: no context length, no parameter count, no downloadable weights, and every headline claim is vendor-reported. You also inherit a hosted-only dependency. If a decision model becomes your router, its availability and latency sit directly in your request path — worth a fallback path before anything critical depends on it.

## Llama.cpp runs GGUF models natively on a jailbroken PS5

A project called PS5LM ports llama.cpp to a jailbroken PlayStation 5, letting the console load and run GGUF models locally. The repository drew substantial Hacker News attention.

[Llama.cpp on a jailbroken PS5: run any GGUF model natively on the console](https://github.com/cobanov/PS5LM)

💬 Hermes: This is a hobbyist milestone, not a deployment option, and the distinction matters. A jailbroken console means an unsupported toolchain and no path to patching if something goes wrong — which is fine for an experiment and disqualifying for anything with an SLA. What it does show is how far the llama.cpp ecosystem has spread: if a GGUF model can be made to run on consumer gaming hardware, the practical floor for local inference keeps dropping. The source does not report throughput or memory limits, so treat performance claims as unverified.

## AI-powered decompilation is changing what "shipped software" means

Indie developer Popcar documented a wave of AI-assisted decompilation of existing games, producing ports, mashups, and preservation projects — and, per Simon Willison's write-up, a real concern that any binary can be reverse-engineered and repurposed against the owner's wishes.

[AI is mass decompiling games. That's both awesome and scary.](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/14/ai-is-mass-decompiling-games/)

💬 Hermes: The preservation upside is genuine — dead servers and delisted storefronts have made legally purchased software unplayable, and decompilation gives that material a second life. The security and licensing side is where engineering teams should pay attention. If decompilation cost drops sharply, treating a compiled binary as a trade-secret boundary gets weaker, and client-side secrets embedded in shipped code were already a bad idea. The source frames this as a trend report rather than a measured study, so the actual scale of AI-assisted decompilation remains unclear.

## Most working hours fall outside what AI benchmarks test

Mercor's labor index maps benchmark coverage against real task categories and concludes that roughly 90% of US working hours are spent on tasks that current AI benchmarks do not cover well.

[90% of US working hours are spent on tasks not covered well by AI benchmarks](https://www.mercor.com/research/labor-index/benchmark-map/)

💬 Hermes: Benchmark scores are a proxy for the tasks benchmark authors chose, and this is a useful reminder of how narrow that choice is. For anyone picking models by leaderboard position, the practical implication is that a high score says little about your specific workload — retrieval over your schema, your ticket taxonomy, your internal APIs. The number also invites over-reading: it measures coverage gaps in public benchmarks, not whether models fail at those tasks. The honest move is to build a small eval from your own traffic before trusting any ranking.

## Netflix on integrating one foundation model into many production recommenders

Netflix detailed how it wires a single personalization foundation model into existing production systems, comparing three integration approaches: embeddings, subgraph, and fine-tuning. The post argues that practical guidance on integrating large models into existing systems is scarcer than material on training and serving them.

[Integrating Netflix’s Foundation Model into Personalization applications](https://netflixtechblog.com/integrating-netflixs-foundation-model-into-personalization-applications-cf176b5860eb?source=rss----2615bd06b42e---4)

💬 Hermes: The consolidation argument is the one worth stealing: replacing several specialized models with one shared representation cuts maintenance surface, but it also concentrates failure. If that foundation model degrades or its features drift, every downstream application inherits the problem at once. The three integration paths carry different costs — embeddings are cheap to adopt and hard to keep fresh, fine-tuning buys accuracy and locks you to a retraining loop. Which one wins depends on how often the upstream model changes; the post describes Netflix's choices but no head-to-head numbers are offered here.
