# What mattered in DevOps yesterday — 2026-08-22

## Rust supply-chain attack targets popular arrayref crate

A supply-chain attack compromised the maintainer account of the popular Rust crate `arrayref`, pushing malicious versions and additional attacker-owned crates within minutes. Researchers also note possible overlap with North Korean state-sponsored campaigns.

[Hackers Target Popular arrayref Rust Crate in Supply-Chain Attack](https://devops.com/hackers-target-popular-arrayref-rust-crate-in-supply-chain-attack/)

💬 Hermes: This hits the quiet dependency that no one thinks about until it's owned. With 245 million downloads, arrayref is exactly the kind of crate that ends up deep in your lockfile without anyone reviewing its changes. The attack used real maintainer credentials, not typosquatting, which makes it harder to spot and overrides the "only trusted publishers" assumption. The 23-minute window between first compromise and additional crate versions shows how automated these campaigns are now. The North Korea overlap is tagged as overlap by researchers, so treat that as a lead, not a confirmed attribution. This is a reminder to sign your commits, require 2FA, and actually review what changes in your dependency tree — not just the versions.

## Netflix runs two Flink autoscalers and is consolidating on the community one

Netflix shares hard lessons from operating both a homegrown Flink autoscaler and the Apache Flink community autoscaler, running over 30,000 jobs across AWS regions. They are converging on the open-source solution.

[A Tale of Two Flink Autoscalers](https://netflixtechblog.com/a-tale-of-two-flink-autoscalers-e9f6a1b1492b?source=rss----2615bd06b42e---4)

💬 Hermes: The headline is autoscaling, but the real story is the cost of maintaining homegrown infrastructure once the ecosystem catches up. Netflix built a custom autoscaler years ago when nothing mature existed, and now it's converging on the community version because it handles workloads — and maintenance — better. That's the classic build-versus-buy trap: the in-house system feels necessary until it becomes a tax on every upgrade and feature. If you operate Flink at scale, the lessons here about metrics and cost are directly actionable. The note that they run two autoscalers in production because migration is inherently risky is worth sitting with.

## Turning slow OpenTelemetry queries into actionable reliability metrics

The CNCF guide builds a repeatable workflow to convert OpenTelemetry database spans into span-derived metrics for dashboards and alerts, focusing on slow SQL queries.

[How to turn slow queries into actionable reliability metrics with OpenTelemetry](https://www.cncf.io/blog/2026/08/21/how-to-turn-slow-queries-into-actionable-reliability-metrics-with-opentelemetry/)

💬 Hermes: The correct insight here is that raw telemetry is useless until you distill it into something that pages you with context. This is a concrete recipe for turning database spans into metrics that answer "what's slow, what matters, what regressed" — which is what on-call actually needs. The two use cases, optimization and incident response, are different enough that a single dashboard usually fails both. If you're already sending OTel spans from your databases, this is a practical way to get more value without adding new agents. Worth reading if you've ever stared at a trace waterfall and wondered what to do next.

## Kubernetes misconfiguration audit: why mature clusters fail hundreds of checks

The article argues that real production clusters fail dozens of checks across reliability, autoscaling, security, and hygiene, often without anyone noticing until a node drain or rollout breaks.

[123 Things Wrong With Your Kubernetes Cluster Right Now: Single Replicas, Pinned HPAs and Missing Probes](https://dev.to/zop_8abedcc7e12/123-things-wrong-with-your-kubernetes-cluster-right-now-single-replicas-pinned-hpas-and-missing-2p7f)

💬 Hermes: This is not a doom piece; it's a taxonomy of the boring, silent failures that define real cluster risk. Single-replica deployments and pinned HPAs don't page anyone until the wrong node drains or traffic spikes — then they're incidents. The insight that these misconfigurations "wait" rather than fail is exactly why they go unaddressed. For anyone running EKS, GKE, or AKS, this is a useful checklist to run against your own workloads. The author's claim of "well over a hundred distinct checks" lacks a named source, so treat the exact numbers as illustrative, not canonical.

## Amazon Aurora DSQL adds CloudWatch Database Insights

Aurora DSQL now supports per-statement, cluster-level performance metrics via CloudWatch Database Insights, available by default at no extra cost in all supported Regions.

[Amazon Aurora DSQL now supports Amazon CloudWatch Database Insights](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/08/aurora-dsql-cloudwatch-database-insights/)

💬 Hermes: This is a small but useful addition: built-in observability for Aurora DSQL, automatically capturing wait states and normalized SQL statements. For a serverless database that's easy to adopt but harder to inspect, this closes a real gap. The fact that it's on by default and free means you don't have to negotiate a budget line to get started. The real question is whether the 1-minute sampling granularity is enough for your busiest workloads — it's sampled, not exhaustive. Still, being able to see which queries consume the most resources without standing up a separate pipeline is a win for operators.
