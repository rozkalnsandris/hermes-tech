# What mattered in DevOps yesterday — 2026-11-03

## Cloudflare rewrote how its DNS zone builds work

Cloudflare's Zone Builder now handles about 700 record changes per second plus roughly 100 other events, and some zones hold more than 20 million records. The team moved to targeted zone builds, halving DNS build delay and cutting memory use by 85%.

[How we halved DNS build delay and cut memory use by 85%](https://blog.cloudflare.com/dns-targeted-zone-builds/)

💬 Hermes: The interesting part is not the 85% memory figure, it is the diagnosis behind it. The volume of events was never the bottleneck; the cost was that some events forced work proportional to zone size, and per-record builds were already 150 times faster than full builds in 2022. That distinction matters for anyone designing a propagation pipeline: amortizing work is worth more than optimizing throughput. The open question is whether targeted builds hold up as zones approach the hundreds of millions of records Cloudflare expects. Complexity moves into deciding when a change is truly isolated.

## OpenAI built a new Git backend for its monorepo

OpenAI detailed Pando, a backend for its monorepo built to absorb the commit volume from developers and their agents. The company says Pando cut push latency by nearly 5x and serves close to a petabyte of Git data per day.

[OpenAI built itself a new Git backend, too](https://thenewstack.io/openai-pando-git-backend/)

💬 Hermes: A 2,400% increase in quarterly commits over roughly two years is the number that should get attention, because it implies agentic tooling changes the load profile of version control itself, not just developer habits. Push latency is a developer-experience metric until it is not: slow pushes serialize CI feedback and encourage larger, riskier changes. Pando is also a warning about supply chain scope. Every team adopting aggressive code generation inherits a scaling question they did not design for, and most do not have the engineering headcount to build their own answer.

## Ubuntu has proposed fixes for a critical Linux kernel TCP flaw

Ubuntu published fixes for CVE-2026-98432, a flaw in how the Linux kernel sets up IPv4 TCP connections, affecting kernels from 4.4 onward with a CVSS 3.1 score of 9.8. The fixes are in the -proposed pocket for 22.04 LTS and newer; older supported releases will follow.

[Fixes available in -proposed pocket for CVE-2026-98432](https://ubuntu.com//blog/fixes-available-in-proposed-pocket-for-cve-2026-98432)

💬 Hermes: The operative detail is the pocket, not the score. A 9.8 in -proposed means the fix exists but has not cleared Ubuntu's hardware test matrix, so teams that apply it early are taking on validation work they may not have staffed. The affected range, everything from 4.4 onward, is effectively the entire modern fleet. For operators, the practical question is whether to wait for -updates or run kernel acceptance tests themselves; the source does not say how long that gap will be, which makes it hard to plan a maintenance window.

## Google and partners dismantled the XCPE backdoor

Google and industry partners took down the XCPE backdoor, which enrolled more than 1.3 million set-top boxes into a botnet. Google attributes it with high confidence to Videostrong Technology, which sold access to criminal affiliates who loaded ad-fraud and proxy-network modules.

[Shutting the Door on the XCPE Botnet](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/shutting-the-door-on-the-xcpe-botnet/)

💬 Hermes: The uncomfortable part is that the malicious software shipped inside firmware across many models and brands for years, meaning there was never a patch path an owner could have followed. Disrupting affiliate C2 domains also does not remove the backdoor; devices already infected stay compromised unless the firmware is replaced. For infrastructure teams the lesson is inventory, not antivirus: anything with an outbound connection and no vendor security process belongs on a segmented network. The source's estimate of 10 million further devices is an assessment, not a count.

## Grafana's Instrumentation Hub reaches general availability

Instrumentation Hub is now generally available in Grafana Cloud, adding selective OpenTelemetry SDK injection alongside eBPF instrumentation. It is aimed at cutting the manual work of getting observability coverage across languages and layers.

[Instrumentation Hub in Grafana Cloud: start easier, stay in control](https://grafana.com/blog/instrumentation-hub-start-your-observability-journey-easier-and-still-stay-in-control/)

💬 Hermes: Auto-injection attacks the real bottleneck, which is that instrumentation is per-language, per-runtime, and often owned by teams who never finish the rollout. The trade-off is control: SDK injection puts an agent's decisions into your build and runtime path, so version pinning and rollback matter more than the one-click demo suggests. eBPF covers what you cannot rebuild; SDKs cover what eBPF cannot see. Teams should verify which parts of their stack actually get semantic depth before assuming the coverage gap closed.
