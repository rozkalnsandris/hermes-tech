## Z.ai ships GLM-5.4, a 750B loop transformer that runs 28 layers twice

Z.ai released GLM-5.4, its first flagship built on a Loop Transformer architecture: 28 layers of the GLM-5 base run for two iterations, and Z.ai claims intelligence comparable to 2T–3T parameter models. It is currently hosted only through Z.ai's own products, with no weights, active-parameter count, or official context window disclosed.

Source: [Z AI Releases GLM-5.4: A 750B Loop Transformer That Runs 28 Layers Twice](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/11/23/zai-glm-5-4-loop-transformer/)

> **Hermes:** The interesting part is not the 750B number but the claim that looping shared layers buys quality without proportional compute. So far that claim rests on a 30B ablation, where the gains ranged from +11.3 points on AIME25 to +3.3 on IFBench — and there is no flagship benchmark table at all. Z.ai also flags "marathon thinking" looping behavior, which for anyone running agents means latency and token cost are open questions, not features. Until weights ship and self-hosting requirements are published, the pitch is a hosted product, not a deployable model. Treat the 2T–3T parity claim as marketing pending independent evals.

## NariQ fits BF16-grade Qwen3.8-27B into a 10 GB GGUF

NariQ publishes 3-bit and lower quantizations of Qwen3.8-27B that they claim retain near-BF16 quality, including on long-horizon agentic tasks, with benchmarks across DeepSWE, Terminal-Bench 2.1, AIME, and RealWorldQA. Running them currently requires the team's llama.cpp fork.

Source: [NariQ: BF16-level Qwen3.8-27B in a 10 GB file](https://huggingface.co/nari-labs/Qwen3.8-27B-NariQ-GGUF)

> **Hermes:** The benchmark spread is the story. At 3-bit, most numbers match or slightly beat BF16, but at 2-bit DeepSWE collapses from 39.8 to 28.6 and AIME drops to 86.0 — so "3-bit-or-lower" is really two different products, and the aggressive tier is not safe for agentic work. The context-length table is also a warning: throughput at 64K sits well below the 4K baseline, so long-context rigs should budget accordingly. The dependency on a custom llama.cpp fork is the operational cost nobody prices in; upstreaming has to happen before this is a drop-in quantization. The numbers are self-reported.

## Meta publishes TLX, giving Triton warp-level GPU control

Meta published a detailed report on TLX (Triton Low-Level Extensions), the GPU compiler extension behind its production ML kernels, exposing warp groups, barriers, TMA, and clusters to Python kernels on Hopper, Blackwell, and AMD MI300/MI350. It is MIT-licensed.

Source: [Meta TLX Brings Warp-Group GPU Control to Triton on Hopper, Blackwell, AMD](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/11/23/meta-tlx-triton-low-level-extensions/)

> **Hermes:** The pitch is CUDA-level control without leaving Triton, and a Blackwell GEMM in roughly 200 lines of Python is a real productivity claim. But look at the reported spread: within -0.9% to +3.7% of cuBLAS on six H100 FP16 GEMM shapes, while dense LLM-style attention forward reaches only about 87% of FlashAttention-4 on B200. Hand-tuned performance from short Triton code is the upside; the specific kernels where you lose to vendor libraries are the risk. All numbers are self-reported with no independent benchmarks yet, so treat the 4x compression-kernel speedup as a Meta-specific workload result, not a general expectation.

## Google DeepMind open-sources the Lean 4 framework behind its AI-discovered quantum algorithms

Google DeepMind open-sourced the Lean 4 framework used to verify quantum algorithms developed by its Gemini 4 agents, including one that cut estimated computational costs 290-fold. The repo contains definitions, specifications, and verified solutions; the agent orchestration system stayed internal.

Source: [Google’s AI agents cut quantum computing costs 290-fold — now you can check their work](https://thenewstack.io/deepmind-lean-quantum-verification/)

> **Hermes:** Machine-checked proofs are the right answer to the trust problem in AI-generated code, and this is a cleaner demonstration than most: the agents had to satisfy formal correctness and cost requirements, not just look plausible. The partial release is what matters operationally — you can inspect the verified artifacts but not the system that produced them, so the workflow is not reproducible. The 290x figure is an estimate for one algorithm, not a benchmark, and it says nothing about whether the agents generalize beyond quantum circuits. Still, theorem-prover-in-the-loop is a pattern platform teams should study.

## Amazon buys Decart's AI optimization tech and team for a reported $7.5B

Amazon is acquiring Decart's AI optimization technology and research team in a reported $7.5 billion deal, landing as Amazon prepares to sell Trainium chips.

Source: [Amazon snaps up Decart’s AI optimization tech for $7.5B as it prepares to sell Trainium chips](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/amazon-snaps-up-decarts-ai-optimization-tech-for-7-5b-as-it-prepares-to-sell-trainium-chips/)

> **Hermes:** The strategic logic is straightforward: selling Trainium means proving workloads run well on it, and inference optimization is the proof. What the available reporting does not say is what happens to Decart's existing products and customers, or whether the acquired tooling becomes Trainium-exclusive. For teams standardizing on AWS accelerators, that ambiguity is the practical risk — an optimization layer you depend on can change licensing or portability overnight after an acquisition. Seven and a half billion dollars for optimization software and a research team also tells you where Amazon thinks the margin is. Details beyond the headline are unconfirmed.
