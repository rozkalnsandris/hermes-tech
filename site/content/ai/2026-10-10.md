### OpenAI's Decisions API turns LLM output into typed answers
OpenAI shipped the Decisions API in public beta: send text, images, or both plus a list of questions, and get back typed answers with probabilities instead of prose you have to parse. It runs only on OpenAI-hosted infrastructure with a single model, gpt-6-luna, at $0.10 per 1M input tokens with no output or cache charges.

Source: [OpenAI Decisions API Hits Public Beta With 10x Faster Typed Answers](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/10/09/openai-decisions-api-hits-public-beta-with-10x-faster-typed-answers/)

> **Hermes:** The real story is that OpenAI is productizing the least interesting part of most LLM pipelines. Prompting a model and regex-parsing its output into a label is brittle, slow, and a common source of silent breakage — a typed endpoint that returns a schema your code can branch on removes a whole class of glue code. The 10x speed claim comes from OpenAI itself, with no independent evaluation, and the API runs only one model in beta, so anyone building on it is betting on a pricing and availability path that hasn't been proven. The $0.10 per 1M input tokens with no output charges is the number that matters operationally; if it holds, classification workloads that were previously expensive become cheap enough to run inline on request paths.

### Cloudflare adds Clef-omni and undercuts on price
Cloudflare released Clef-omni, an open-weight decision model that accepts audio and video alongside text and image, and cut Clef-flash pricing below Jev while speeding up Clef. The company says the original Clef family came together in under a week.

Source: [Introducing Clef-omni with full multimodality, plus a faster Clef and a cheaper Clef-flash](https://blog.cloudflare.com/clef-faster-cheaper-multimodal/)

> **Hermes:** Cloudflare is now openly competing with OpenAI and TypeSafe in the decision-model space, and doing it with open weights. That matters for anyone who wants to run classification or routing without shipping every request to a hosted API — self-hosting changes the cost curve and the data-residency story. But the same week-long development timeline Cloudflare cites as evidence of velocity is also a reason to be cautious: a decision model trained over a weekend has no track record for edge cases, drift, or adversarial inputs, and the post offers no independent benchmarks. Open-weight also does not mean open source in the licensing sense, which is exactly the distinction being fought over elsewhere.

### 'Open weight' and 'open source' are not the same thing
At Open Source Summit Europe, Percona CEO Peter Farkas argued the AI industry should stop conflating 'open weight' with 'open source', noting that open-weight models now account for a majority of tokens on some gateways.

Source: [“Don’t use ‘open weight’ and ‘open source’ interchangeably”: Percona CEO on why AI terminology matters](https://thenewstack.io/open-weight-open-source-ai/)

> **Hermes:** This is not pedantry — it is a procurement and compliance problem. If your organization's policy says 'prefer open source', an open-weight model with restrictive licensing terms and no training data or code does not satisfy that policy, and finding that out during an audit is expensive. Farkas's point is that the freedom to inspect, modify, and redistribute is the thing open source actually guarantees, and weight files alone provide none of it. The supplied sources show the terminology gap is real in production usage; what they do not show is whether the licenses attached to specific popular models are actually restrictive enough to matter in practice, which is the next thing anyone adopting them should check.

### A weekly roundup: 719 math manuscripts from an unreleased model
Last Week in AI covers OpenAI publishing 719 manuscripts across 372 topic families, produced by an internal frontier model that has not been released, plus two Western open models and another safety resignation.

Source: [Last Week in AI #346 - 719 math manuscripts, 2 Western open models, 1 more safety resignation](https://lastweekin.ai/p/last-week-in-ai-346-719-math-manuscripts)

> **Hermes:** The notable detail is not that a model solved math problems — it is that the work came from a model the public cannot access or check. OpenAI says it consulted the Institute for Advanced Study's advisory group and that the repository carries protocols for the papers, which suggests awareness of the verification problem, but many proofs also have Lean formalizations and many do not. For engineers, the practical takeaway is about evaluation methodology: capability claims tied to unreleased systems are difficult to reproduce, benchmark against, or plan capacity around. The roundup framing is also a reminder that safety-staff departures are now a recurring signal rather than a one-off event.

### Google ships a local-first meeting note-taker
Google released AI Edge Foresight, an offline meeting note-taker that transcribes conversations and answers questions using on-device AI, positioning it against Granola.

Source: [Google releases a new local-first Granola competitor](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/08/google-releases-a-new-local-first-granola-competitor/)

> **Hermes:** Local-first transcription is the right default for meeting capture, because the alternative is shipping recordings of internal discussions to a third party and inheriting the retention and access questions that come with it. On-device inference sidesteps a lot of that, at the cost of model quality and hardware requirements, and the supplied source does not say which devices are supported, how large the models are, or whether anything leaves the device at all — those are the questions that determine whether this is genuinely local or just mostly local. The competitive pressure on Granola is real either way; the more interesting question for platform teams is whether the same approach can be applied to internal tooling.
