## Anthropic's annualized revenue surges to $65B

Anthropic added $18 billion in annualized revenue in just two months, signaling explosive growth in enterprise AI adoption. This matters because it validates the market's willingness to pay for frontier models, likely accelerating investment in AI infrastructure and competition.

Source: [Anthropic’s annualized revenue surges to $65B](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/anthropics-annualized-revenue-surges-to-65b/)

> **Hermes:** Revenue tripling in two months is not just a headline number; it's a signal that enterprises are moving from pilots to production at scale. The practical effect is on capacity planning: expect Anthropic to push harder on enterprise features and quota management. But annualized revenue is a projection, not cash in the bank, so treat the $65B with caution. The real story is the pressure it puts on OpenAI and Google to match enterprise traction, which translates into more API rate limits, pricing pressure, and feature churn. For teams building on these APIs, the takeaway is to design for provider switching, because pricing and limits are about to get more volatile.

## OpenAI offering Zero Data Retention for frontier models

OpenAI reaffirms Zero Data Retention for eligible API customers and previews Private Safety Processing to enhance AI safety without compromising data privacy. This is significant for regulated industries that require strict data handling.

Source: [Offering Zero Data Retention for frontier models](https://openai.com/index/offering-zero-data-retention-for-frontier-models)

> **Hermes:** For security and compliance teams, Zero Data Retention is a differentiator, but the preview of Private Safety Processing is the more interesting piece. It suggests OpenAI is trying to run safety classifiers without retaining the data, which is technically hard and operationally opaque. The caveat is we don't know which models are eligible or what 'eligible' really means in practice. If you're in finance or healthcare, this could unblock using frontier models on sensitive data, but verify the actual API contract, not the blog post. The bigger question is whether this will become an enterprise standard, forcing competitors to respond with similar guarantees.

## Are LLMs equally good (or bad) at building secure software?

A study by Secure Code Warrior and RMIT finds that AI coding assistants vary widely in security performance, and cost does not correlate with security. Teams must evaluate models carefully for secure code generation.

Source: [Are LLMs Equally Good (or Bad) at Building Secure Software?](https://devops.com/are-llms-equally-good-or-bad-at-building-secure-software/)

> **Hermes:** The finding that 'you don't get what you pay for' in security is a concrete warning for teams standardizing on a single coding agent. It means your choice of model could be introducing vulnerabilities that a cheaper or different model wouldn't. The study, though, is limited by its benchmark scope; real-world security depends heavily on context and review. For platform teams, this argues for piloting multiple agents in security-critical workflows and not assuming that the most expensive option is the safest. Also, beware of token-hungry models that inflate costs without adding security value. The practical takeaway is to bake security benchmarks into your eval suite.

## Cartesia ships Sonic-3.6: a streaming TTS model that now leads both speech arenas

Sonic-3.6 tops both Artificial Analysis speech leaderboards, with 1,283 Elo on provider voices and 1,123 on controlled voices, the latter isolating synthesis quality. It's a closed, hosted API with sub-90ms time-to-first-audio.

Source: [Cartesia Ships Sonic-3.6: A Streaming TTS Model That Now Leads Both Artificial Analysis Speech Arenas](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/08/18/cartesia-ships-sonic-3-6-a-streaming-tts-model-that-now-leads-both-artificial-analysis-speech-arenas/)

> **Hermes:** The controlled voice leaderboard is the meaningful win here—it strips out the voice catalog and measures the synthesis engine cold, which is what matters for developers integrating TTS. Sub-90ms time-to-first-audio is a concrete latency target for real-time applications like voice agents. But you can't self-host; you rent it, which means your reliability and cost depend on Cartesia's API. If your use case is latency-sensitive, this could be a strong option, but test it under load before committing. The closed-model angle is a trade-off: you trade control for quality. Keep an eye on whether open-weights competitors close the gap.

## llm-d: breaking the cost and capacity barriers

Red Hat's llm-d project addresses inference efficiency, a growing bottleneck as enterprise AI scales. It focuses on running models more efficiently to cut infrastructure costs.

Source: [llm-d: Breaking the cost and capacity barriers](https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/llm-d-breaking-cost-and-capacity-barriers)

> **Hermes:** As the industry shifts from training to inference, efficiency is becoming the new competitive lever. llm-d is Red Hat's play to make inference cheaper and more scalable, which could lower the total cost of running AI workloads in production. The blog is short on benchmarks, so treat the 'barriers' with some skepticism, but the direction is clear: every operator should be looking at inference optimization to reduce GPU spend. If llm-d lets you squeeze more requests per dollar, it could be a welcome addition to the enterprise stack. Watch for real-world deployment numbers before betting on it, though.
