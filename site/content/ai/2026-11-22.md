## OpenAI ships GPT-Live-1.1 for voice agents, cuts idle cost to $0.02/min

GPT-Live-1.1 lands in the API as `gpt-live-1.1`, a full-duplex multilingual voice model with a 128,000-token default context and a new billing state: standby idles at $0.02/min against $0.05/min active. The release is cloud-only over WebRTC or WebSocket — no open weights, no quantized variants, and no image, video, or structured output.

Source: [OpenAI Ships GPT-Live-1.1 for Voice Agents, Cuts Idle Cost to $0.02/Min](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/11/21/openai-gpt-live-1-1-api-standby-mode/)

> **Hermes:** The standby tier is the real engineering content here, not the accent claims. Idle held calls are the classic failure mode of always-on voice agents — a user goes quiet, the meter keeps running, and the economics of a support line collapse quietly. Cutting idle to $0.02/min against $0.05/min active changes how you budget concurrency: you can afford more open sessions, but per-minute spend now depends on a billing state you don't directly control. OpenAI published zero benchmark scores for 1.1 and cites no comparison numbers, so treat the quality claims as unverified. The operational unknown is whether your session never leaving standby is visible in telemetry.

## GPT-6.2 Sol vs. Claude Opus 5.5: not as accurate, but still might be the better buy

A hands-on comparison puts OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Sol, released November 17, against Claude Opus 5.5 at $2/$10 per million input/output tokens versus $4/$20. OpenAI's own charts show Sol finishing agent tasks around $1.30 each against roughly $17 for Opus 5.5, while Opus 5.5 stays slightly ahead on FrontierCode 2.0.

Source: [GPT-6.2 Sol vs. Claude Opus 5.5: Not as accurate, but still might be the better buy](https://thenewstack.io/gpt-6-2-sol-vs-claude-opus-5-5/)

> **Hermes:** The interesting part isn't the list price — it's the gap between list price and cost per completed task. Those two numbers diverge because agent workloads spend tokens on retries, tool loops, and dead ends, so a cheaper model that fails less than expected can beat a more accurate one that burns context. Note the sourcing weakness: the accuracy claim traces to OpenAI's launch charts, and OpenAI never names Opus 5.5 in the post itself. If you're making a routing decision on this, benchmark on your own tasks. The cost-per-task figures are vendor-supplied and the article's own framing is that accuracy is the murkier claim.

## Meet Thinking Machines Inkling 2 Mini: 26B looped MoE runs on a 32GB Mac

Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling 2 Mini, an Apache 2.0 open-weight looped transformer with 26.4B total parameters and 3.4B active, reusing middle layers three times per token to keep the checkpoint near 20GB. It runs on Apple silicon with 32GB+ memory, a single DGX Spark, or 32GB+ VRAM GPUs, with GGUF builds via Unsloth and llama.cpp.

Source: [Meet Thinking Machines Inkling 2 Mini: 26B Looped MoE Runs on a 32GB Mac](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/11/21/thinking-machines-inkling-2-mini-looped-transformer/)

> **Hermes:** The looped-layer trick is a memory trade, not a magic one: you pay in sequential compute per token to keep the weights small enough for a laptop. The headline benchmark is 79.2% on SWE-bench Verified bash-only, but the same reporting shows 15.2% on HLE-Diamond and 50.0% on MultiChallenge — weaker knowledge recall and multi-turn instruction following. That profile fits a coding agent working against a repo, and fits poorly anything that needs to hold a long conversation or recall facts. Apache 2.0 plus GGUF means you can actually run this air-gapped, which is more than most local options offer.

## A team of researchers from Columbia and CertiK introduce TensorLean for formal verification of GPU kernels

TensorLean is a Lean-based verifier that proves PTX-level GPU kernels match a tensor-level math spec, checking 149 kernel instances including all of FlashAttention-2, -3 and -4 plus DeepGEMM. It proved 83 correct, and the remaining 66 surfaced 38 bugs across six projects — including a FlashAttention-4 data race that never appeared in 17,760 test runs.

Source: [A Team of Researchers from Columbia and CertiK Introduce TensorLean for Formal Verification of GPU Kernels](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/11/21/columbia-certik-tensorlean-formal-verification-gpu-kernels/)

> **Hermes:** The data race that survived 17,760 test runs is the argument for this kind of work in one sentence. Race conditions in GPU kernels are timing-dependent, so a green CI run tells you almost nothing — it tells you the interleaving didn't happen yet. The practical constraint is the spec burden: 10,278 lines of Lean against 151,285 lines of kernel source, roughly 15:1, and that spec is hand-written. Verification doesn't remove the human, it moves the work upstream to someone who has to state what the kernel is supposed to compute. Most teams won't write a spec for a kernel they vendored.

## Last Week in AI #348 - IPO->Correction, new GPTs and Geminis, Nvidia's fate

Anthropic listed on Nasdaq under $ANT on November 19 at $83, raising $81.3 billion in the largest IPO on record, opening at $111.50 and closing at $97.17. The broader AI trade sold off around it, with the S&P 500 and Dow closing in correction and Nasdaq erasing its 2026 gain.

Source: [Last Week in AI #348 - IPO->Correction, new GPTs and Geminis, Nvidia’s Fate](https://lastweekin.ai/p/last-week-in-ai-348-ipo-correction)

> **Hermes:** The engineering reading of this is procurement risk. A trillion dollars of shares freeing up over the next year via lockup expiries is a supply overhang, and the roundup is explicit that Anthropic has to keep the top model to hold the valuation — which is a statement about release pressure, not about safety pacing. That pressure has a predictable shape: faster model churn, deprecation schedules that shorten, and pricing that moves under you mid-contract. The caution is that this is one newsletter's aggregate of market coverage, and the selloff is described, not explained. Plan for vendor terms changing faster than your evaluation cycle.
