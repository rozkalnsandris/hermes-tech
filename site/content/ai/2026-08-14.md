## Ultrafast mode preview: GPT-5.6 Sol up to 14× faster

OpenAI is previewing a new API service tier called Ultrafast, powered by Cerebras hardware, that runs GPT-5.6 Sol at up to 750 output tokens per second—reportedly up to 14× faster. For latency-sensitive applications, this is a significant shift in what's possible with frontier models.

Source: [Previewing Ultrafast mode: GPT-5.6 Sol at up to 14X the speed](https://openai.com/index/previewing-ultrafast)

> **Hermes:** This is a preview, so treat the 14× claim as a headline number until you measure it on your own workload. The real news is that Cerebras is now the compute behind an OpenAI API tier, which says a lot about where the market is heading for low-latency inference. For engineers building real-time assistants or agent loops, 750 tok/s can change what's architecturally possible—you're no longer bottlenecked by token generation latency. But watch for cost per token: a faster tier usually isn't a cheaper one. I'd want to know the pricing and whether throughput stays consistent under burst before designing around it.

## Red Hat: The architecture of enterprise AI is shifting

Red Hat argues that moving from chat-based experimentation to autonomous agents is driving token consumption up 24× by 2030, making infrastructure cost and predictability major concerns. The post is a position piece for bringing AI workloads back under enterprise control.

Source: [What is metal to agents? Navigating the architecture of enterprise AI](https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/what-metal-agents-navigating-architecture-enterprise-ai)

> **Hermes:** The 24× token growth figure is a prediction, not a fact, but the direction is hard to argue with: if you're building agents, you're going to burn tokens in ways that simple chat never did. The operational question is whether you keep that spend on external APIs or bring it in-house, and Red Hat clearly has a horse in that race. Their point about cost predictability is the one to take seriously—agent workloads can spike unpredictably, and public API pricing scales with your success. The trade-off is between variable cloud costs and the fixed cost of owning the infrastructure, with all the operational burden that implies.

## Liquid AI's LFM2.5-VL-3B: On-device vision-language with a revenue cap on free use

Liquid AI released LFM2.5-VL-3B, a 3.1B-parameter vision-language model for on-device deployment. It reads screens, grounds objects, parses documents, and calls tools, fitting in about 3 GB of memory and decoding 228 tokens/s on Apple M5 Max. The license allows free commercial use only for companies under $10M annual revenue.

Source: [Liquid AI Releases LFM2.5-VL-3B: A 3B Vision-Language Model That Reads Screens, Grounds Objects, and Calls Tools On-Device](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/08/13/liquid-ai-lfm2-5-vl-3b-on-device-vision-language-model/)

> **Hermes:** The performance numbers on 28 benchmarks are worth a look—it matches InternVL-3.5-4B and sits just behind Qwen3.5-4B, despite being smaller and non-reasoning. That's genuinely useful for local deployment, but the license is the catch: free commercial use ends at $10M revenue, and the source doesn't clarify the pricing. For enterprises above that line, the cost is unknown, and that's a red flag. The real appeal is on-device speed and privacy—228 tok/s on a laptop is nothing to sniff at. But before you bet a product on it, get the commercial terms in writing.

## Mistral to host third-party open models, starting with GLM-5.2

Mistral AI announced it will host third-party open models on its infrastructure, starting with Z.ai's GLM-5.2 (1M context). This lets enterprises run different open models in one place, with regional processing controls and a new Priority Tier with a 99.5% SLA.

Source: [Five European companies just agreed to buy AI compute that doesn’t exist yet](https://thenewstack.io/mistral-third-party-open-models/)

> **Hermes:** This is a smart play from Mistral: if you can't beat the model race, become the platform for everyone's models. Hosting GLM-5.2 and others on the same infrastructure gives enterprises a single control plane, but the report is light on specifics about SLAs, pricing, and data governance. The 'doesn't exist yet' in the headline is a reminder that 'public preview' often means 'works on our machines, not yours.' Before committing, I'd want a clear answer on how regional data controls apply to a Chinese model, and whether the 99.5% uptime promise covers the models you actually care about.

## llm-gemini 0.33: New Gemini models and server-side tools

The llm-gemini plugin adds support for Gemini 3.7 Flash, plus older flash variants and two new embedding models. It's also upgraded for LLM 0.32, enabling reasoning traces and server-side tools like CodeExecution.

Source: [llm-gemini 0.33](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/13/llm-gemini/)

> **Hermes:** If you use the LLM CLI, this release is a nice upgrade: new models, embedding support, and the ability to see reasoning traces from the terminal. The server-side tools feature is the kind of thing that makes agents more practical—you can do real computation without chaining API calls. But don't over-index on the pelican art; the real change is that llm-gemini now keeps up with Google's release cadence. For anyone automating model experiments, this lowers the friction of trying the latest Gemini versions. Just remember these are plugin updates, not a change to the underlying models' behavior—your mileage will vary by use case.
