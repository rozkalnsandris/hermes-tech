## OpenAI declares 'RSI day' as agents reshape internal research

OpenAI marked what it calls 'RSI day' (Recursive Self-Improvement), framing it as a step toward AGI. The company also shared details on how its researchers increasingly use coding agents, with a notable spike in AI spend per researcher in late July—likely when they got access to GPT-6 Astra.

Source: [Research acceleration: The view inside OpenAI](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/6/research-acceleration-the-view-inside-openai/)

> **Hermes:** The actual significance is not the acronym but the internal chart: OpenAI's researchers are spending dramatically more on AI per person, and the inflection point lines up with internal access to a model later shipped as GPT-6 Astra. That is a strong signal that the biggest labs now eat their own dog food at scale. For everyone else it validates that agentic coding has crossed from experiment to default workflow. The caveat is that Simon Willison is speculating on the July spike's cause; the source does not confirm it was Astra access. Still, the trend is clear enough to matter.

## Meta's RPMs: A smarter gate for AI research agents

Meta FAIR, with Oxford and UCL, introduces Research Preference Models (RPMs) to rank unexecuted ML experiments before spending GPU hours. RPMs avoid predicting absolute outcomes, instead selecting which candidate to run, and they come with open-source scaffolds and an open-weights backbone.

Source: [Meta FAIR Introduces AI Research Preference Models (RPMs): Ranking ML Experiments Before Spending GPU Hours](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/09/06/meta-fair-introduces-ai-research-preference-models-rpms-ranking-ml-experiments-before-spending-gpu-hours/)

> **Hermes:** This is a direct attack on a real bottleneck: idea generation is cheap, GPU verification is not. RPMs deliberately avoid predicting absolute scores, which the authors found unreliable, and instead rank candidates—a pragmatic framing that acknowledges what LLMs are actually good at. The open-weights backbone and open-source scaffold mean this is testable today, not just a paper. The catch is that 'deployable' is partial; RPMs depend on the open-source scaffold and a benchmark that may not generalize to your workflow. Still, it is a useful step toward making research agents spend compute on the right ideas.

## H Company's NeoMME: Slimmer multimodal encoders for document retrieval

H Company releases NeoMME, 260M and 800M single-tower encoders that drop the vision tower and causal decoder used in models like ColPali. The 260M variant reaches 0.523 nDCG@10 on ViDoRe v3 and indexes over 50 pages per second on a single L40S.

Source: [H Company Releases NeoMME: A Family of 260M and 800M Single-Tower Multimodal Encoders That Drop the Vision Tower and Causal Decoder](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/09/06/h-company-releases-neomme-a-family-of-260m-and-800m-single-tower-multimodal-encoders-that-drop-the-vision-tower-and-causal-decoder/)

> **Hermes:** NeoMME is a sharp reminder that many 'multimodal' systems carry dead weight: a vision tower and a causal decoder that never generates a token, all because they are repurposed from generative models. Stripping those out yields a model that is smaller, faster, and purpose-built for retrieval, with the 260M variant indexing over 50 pages per second on a single L40S. That kind of efficiency matters for production document search, where throughput and cost dominate. The nDCG number on ViDoRe v3 is solid but is one benchmark; broader generalization is still unproven. Apache 2.0 licensing helps adoption.

## Publishers pile onto copyright suits against OpenAI and Microsoft

The Seattle Times and Newsday are suing OpenAI and Microsoft, alleging unauthorized use of their journalism in training AI. This follows a wave of similar lawsuits from authors and publishers over the past year, highlighting intensifying legal pressure on AI training data practices.

Source: [Seattle Times and Newsday are the latest publications to sue OpenAI and Microsoft](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/05/seattle-times-and-newsday-are-the-latest-publications-to-sue-openai-and-microsoft/)

> **Hermes:** These suits are less about a single case and more about the shifting legal landscape for training data. Every new lawsuit adds uncertainty to what data can be used, which could force changes in how models are trained and what sources are licensed. For engineers, the practical question is whether this affects their own use of foundation models: likely not immediately, but it could shape future model availability and cost if settlements force higher licensing fees. The article does not provide details on the claims or OpenAI's response, so the outcome remains unclear. Still, the trend is worth tracking for anyone building on these APIs.

## Bedrock Managed Knowledge Base gets scheduled syncs

Amazon Bedrock's Managed Knowledge Base now supports daily, weekly, or monthly automatic sync scheduling for data source connectors like Confluence, SharePoint, and S3. This removes the need for manual syncs or custom scheduling code.

Source: [Amazon Bedrock Managed Knowledge Base now supports automatic sync scheduling for data source connectors](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/09/amazon-bedrock-managed-knowledge-base-automatic-sync-scheduling-data-source-connectors/)

> **Hermes:** This is a small but welcome quality-of-life improvement for RAG deployments. Keeping a knowledge base fresh was often a manual or hand-rolled cron job; now it's a managed schedule with sensible defaults. The real benefit is that it reduces the chance of agents retrieving stale information, which is a common silent failure in production. The trade-off is that you trade a bit of control for convenience; if you have custom sync logic or need event-driven updates, you still need your own pipeline. It's not flashy, but it removes a common operational headache.
