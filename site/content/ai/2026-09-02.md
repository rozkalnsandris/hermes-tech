## Claude Fable 5.1 arrives with a big science jump and a pelican problem

Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.1 (and Mythos 5.1), claiming a new standard for coding and knowledge work. The headline number is a 52.6% on the new Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1, up from 24.7% for Fable 5 — but other benchmarks show only modest gains.

Source: [Claude Fable 5.1 made me a really nice animated pelican](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/1/claude-fable-5-1/)

> **Hermes:** The scientific reasoning jump is impressive on paper, but watch the benchmark. Terminal-Bench-Science is brand new, and the pelican test shows that Fable 5.1's edge is inconsistent across tasks and reasoning levels. For engineering work, the more relevant change may be the five reasoning levels and the removal of a no-reasoning option — that affects latency and cost in production. The source also notes the long-running task improvements, which is where agents actually earn their keep. The real question is whether the science gains carry over to messy real-world codebases.

## OpenAI's Astra crosses critical cybersecurity threshold

OpenAI announced that Astra is the first model to meet the 'Critical' cybersecurity capability threshold under its Preparedness Framework, and that it ships with stronger safeguards. The implication is that Astra's cyber capabilities are advanced enough to demand extra caution.

Source: [Path to Astra: critical capabilities and frontier safeguards](https://openai.com/index/path-to-astra)

> **Hermes:** This is more than a model release. OpenAI is effectively telling us that Astra can do real damage in the wrong hands, which is why they've tightened safeguards for release. For security teams, that's a signal to evaluate the model's dual-use potential seriously before deploying it in environments where prompt injection or misuse could have real consequences. The Preparedness Framework threshold is a useful risk indicator, but it's self-assessed — the actual behavior in the wild is still unproven. The source does not detail what those stronger safeguards actually are.

## GLM-5.3-Flash: 16x cheaper, but does it hold up?

The New Stack compared Z.AI's GLM-5.3-Flash against its flagship GLM-5.3. Flash is dramatically cheaper on paper, but the real test is token usage — if Flash needs more tokens to get the same job done, the cost advantage could shrink.

Source: [GLM-5.3-Flash vs. GLM-5.3: Time and money, not the spec sheet](https://thenewstack.io/glm-flash-flagship-benchmark/)

> **Hermes:** The 16x price gap is a classic bait-and-switch risk: a cheaper token price hides a model that talks more to get the same answer. The New Stack's tests are a good start, but 27 questions is a small sample for production workloads. The deeper issue is the architectural change — 3x less attention computation means a real trade-off in reasoning depth, which may show up in complex, multi-step tasks. For engineering teams, the practical move is to benchmark against your own prompts, not the marketing sheet.

## llama.cpp gets a boost for old AMD GPUs

A community fork of llama.cpp for AMD gfx906 (Radeon VII, MI50, MI60) reports a +14.1% improvement in prompt processing and +9.3% in long-context fill over upstream. The gains come from reworked Flash Attention and adaptive native/convert selection.

Source: [Update: llama.cpp for Radeon VII / MI50 / MI60 — +14% PP, +9% long-context fill vs upstream + adaptive Flash Attention](https://www.reddit.com/r/LocalLLaMA/comments/1w4d52c/update_llamacpp_for_radeon_vii_mi50_mi60_14_pp_9/)

> **Hermes:** For anyone running older AMD GPUs in production, this is a meaningful upgrade — those cards are still common in some inference clusters. The benchmarks are solid, and the byte-for-byte deterministic output is a good sign for reproducibility. But it's a community fork, not upstream, so you're on the hook for maintenance and compatibility. The improvements are specific to gfx906, so they won't help if you're on newer hardware. Watch for regressions when you update.

## 44% on ARC-AGI-1 for 67 cents

A developer achieved a 44% score on the ARC-AGI-1 benchmark for only 67 cents in compute, sparking discussion on Hacker News about cost-efficient reasoning.

Source: [44% on ARC-AGI-1 in 67 cents](https://mvakde.github.io/blog/44-on-arc-1/)

> **Hermes:** This matters because it shows that frontier-level reasoning might not require enormous compute budgets. For engineers, the implication is that cost-efficient inference with clever prompting or smaller models could change the economics of building AI features. But 44% is still far from human-level reasoning, and ARC-AGI-1 is a narrow benchmark. It's unclear whether this approach generalizes to real-world tasks. Still, it's a useful data point for anyone deciding how to allocate compute between model size and inference strategy.
