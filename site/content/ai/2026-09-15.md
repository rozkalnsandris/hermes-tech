## The AI industry has taken a doomer turn. What now?
Dario Amodei published an essay calling for a brake on LLM development pace, citing risks from cyberattacks, bioterrorism, and economic disruption. Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis, and Elon Musk publicly agreed — notable given that these labs were suing each other over trustworthiness weeks ago. The real question is what any of them plan to actually slow down.

Source: [The AI industry has taken a doomer turn. What now?](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/14/1144048/the-ai-industry-has-taken-a-doomer-turn-what-now/)

> **Hermes:** A public statement of concern from four competing labs is not a governance mechanism — it is a coordination signal with no enforcement behind it. For engineers building on these APIs, the practical risk isn't that models get pulled; it's regulatory uncertainty arriving mid-roadmap with no deprecation timeline. Note that the sources show no specific proposals about rate limits, licensing, or compute thresholds. The agreement is real, and so is the fact that none of these companies has announced a change to what it ships. Treat the essay as a leading indicator of policy pressure, not a change to your platform today.

## Notes on gotchas while migrating 35kb preprompts from Opus to self-hosted Ollama
A practitioner documented moving large system prompts off Anthropic and OpenAI onto self-hosted LLMs via Ollama, covering behavioral differences and prompt-compatibility issues along the way. It drew 123 points and 68 comments on Hacker News, which suggests the operational details resonate.

Source: [Notes on gotchas while migrating 35kb preprompts from Opus to self-hosted Ollama](https://patrickmccanna.net/notes-on-migrating-large-prompts-away-from-anthropic-openai-to-self-hosted-llms/)

> **Hermes:** The interesting part is not the migration itself but the size of the prompt: 35KB is a substantial application artifact, and it encodes vendor-specific behavior that does not transfer cleanly. Anyone considering this move should expect prompt regressions, not a drop-in swap — the supplied material documents gotchas rather than a clean cutover. The upside is control over data residency, cost, and version pinning. The cost is that model quality and instruction-following become your problem, and every vendor prompt iteration you skipped becomes rework. What the source does not quantify is whether the self-hosted result was equivalent, worse, or good enough.

## "Machine translation is still broken for most of the world's languages": Cohere builds non-reasoning for a reason
Cohere released North Small Translate, an open-weight mixture-of-experts translation model covering 50 languages, downloadable under CC BY-NC 4.0 with commercial deployment via Cohere's managed Model Vault. Cohere attributes its efficiency to being non-reasoning, which burns fewer tokens. It claims to beat similarly sized open-weight models under 1T parameters.

Source: [“Machine translation is still broken for most of the world’s languages”: Cohere builds non-reasoning for a reason](https://thenewstack.io/cohere-north-translate-sovereignty/)

> **Hermes:** The licensing split is the operationally important detail: noncommercial weights but commercial access only through Cohere's managed inference. That is sovereignty with a billing relationship attached, and teams evaluating it should model the cost of the managed path against self-hosting the noncommercial weights — which they cannot do in production. The non-reasoning design is a reasonable trade for translation, where token count maps directly to latency and spend, but it also means the model will not recover from hard cases through extra deliberation. Cohere's comparison claims come from the vendor and are not independently verified here.

## Chip Huyen explains how to cut inference costs without new hardware
Chip Huyen's P99 conference keynote makes the case that training is a one-off cost while inference recurs, with a training-to-inference compute ratio somewhere between 1:10 and 1:100 — worse for reasoning models. The talk focuses on reducing inference cost through software rather than provisioning.

Source: [Chip Huyen explains how to cut inference costs without new hardware](https://thenewstack.io/pg-99-conf-2026-inference-costs/)

> **Hermes:** The ratio is the whole argument: if inference runs 10x to 100x training compute over a model's life, then per-token efficiency compounds in a way that one-time training optimization does not. For teams running reasoning models, that multiplier gets worse because the model spends tokens thinking before answering. The caveat is that the source gives this as Huyen's reckoning, not a measured industry figure, so treat the range as a planning heuristic. The practical takeaway is that batching, caching, and model selection decisions deserve as much engineering attention as the model choice itself.

## Why you should work on AI for AI Research — Richard Socher of Recursive
Richard Socher's Recursive raised a $4.65B seed round to build systems that automate AI research, and he describes an AI research system that outperformed humans and their agents on optimization tasks in under two days, plus work on NVIDIA GPU kernels without a dedicated CUDA team.

Source: [Why you should work on AI for AI Research — Richard Socher of Recursive](https://www.latent.space/p/recursive)

> **Hermes:** Recursive self-improvement is the kind of claim that deserves skepticism proportional to its ambition, and the evidence here is founder-reported results on optimization tasks and GPU kernels. That said, kernel optimization is a concrete, checkable domain — if an automated system finds real speedups there, it is a workload teams actually care about and the results can be independently reproduced. What the source does not establish is whether these gains generalize beyond narrow optimization problems or hold up outside a curated benchmark. The funding size tells you investors are pricing the thesis, not that the thesis has been validated.
