## DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731: a quiet upgrade that shifts the frontier

DeepSeek released DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, a post-training update to its V4-Flash model, and moved the API to public beta. The architecture and size haven't changed, but agentic and coding gains suggest the company is focusing on making existing models more useful rather than chasing raw scale.

Source: [DeepSeek Upgrades DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 with Major Agentic and Coding Gains](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/07/31/deepseek-upgrades-deepseek-v4-flash-0731-with-major-agentic-and-coding-gains/)

> **Hermes:** A post-training bump without architecture changes is the cheapest way to move the frontier, and it tells you where the leverage is right now. The gains are in agentic behavior and coding, which are exactly the tasks that matter for tool use in production. The model card says it supersedes the preview, but the supplied source doesn't include benchmarks or what changed in the training data, so treat the claims as directionally useful rather than rigorously verified. For teams already on V4-Flash, this is a low-risk upgrade path; for anyone evaluating, the question is whether the API beta holds up under real load. The unchanged architecture keeps the same cost profile, so the value is purely in capability per dollar.

## OpenAI disrupts a Cambodia-based scam operation

OpenAI took action against a Cambodia-based operation that used ChatGPT for investment, romance, gambling, and impersonation scams. By disrupting the accounts and infrastructure, the company is flexing its ability to police malicious use of its own platform.

Source: [Disrupting a Criminal Scam Operation](https://openai.com/index/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-criminal-scam-operation)

> **Hermes:** This is a rare concrete example of an AI provider exercising real control over abusive usage, not just publishing policy. For engineers, the interesting part is the mechanism: how do you detect and shut down coordinated scam activity across accounts without breaking legitimate uses? The source is brief and doesn't explain the detection methods, so the operational details are unclear. The broader implication is that providers are now in the business of adversarial takedowns, which adds a security-operations burden to running AI platforms. Expect more of these announcements as abuse grows, but also expect them to remain vague on specifics to avoid tipping off bad actors.

## Running Kimi K3 on 29 GB of RAM at 0.50 tok/s

A GitHub project shows how to run the Kimi K3 open-weight model using just 29 GB of RAM, albeit at a very slow 0.50 tokens per second. The hack likely relies on aggressive quantization and offloading, making it possible to experiment with a frontier-class model on consumer hardware.

Source: [Run Kimi K3 using 29 GB of RAM at 0.50 tok/s](https://github.com/sqliteai/waste)

> **Hermes:** The number 0.50 tok/s tells you everything: this is a curiosity, not a practical deployment. But it matters because it proves the open-weight ecosystem is moving toward running serious models on commodity hardware, which changes what's possible for local experimentation and privacy-sensitive workloads. The practical constraint is obvious—nobody wants to wait twenty minutes for a single answer—so the real value is in validation and testing, not interactive use. The source doesn't specify the quantization format or trade-offs, so accuracy may be degraded. Still, this is a useful data point for anyone planning offline or air-gapped inference.

## Snapchat stops rewarding fully AI-generated Spotlight content

Snapchat changed its recommendation systems to exclude fully AI-generated videos from Spotlight, aiming to reduce 'AI slop' on the platform. The move signals a broader industry shift toward curating for human-made content.

Source: [Snapchat no longer rewards fully AI-generated Spotlight content](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/31/snapchat-no-longer-rewards-fully-ai-generated-spotlight-content/)

> **Hermes:** Snapchat is drawing a line between AI-assisted and AI-only creation, which is a policy decision that many platforms are wrestling with. The technical challenge is detecting whether a video is 'fully' AI-generated, especially as tools become more realistic and blend with human editing. The source doesn't explain how the detection works, so there's uncertainty about false positives and circumvention. For creators, this could affect revenue if they rely on AI pipelines. Broader, it's another sign that the economics of content platforms are shifting to reward human effort and authenticity.

## Google Earth's AI deepfake tool pulled after one day

Google shut down its AI image generation feature in Google Earth that let users edit satellite images with text prompts. The tool was effectively a deepfake generator for real-world locations, raising obvious misuse concerns.

Source: [Google Earth&#8217;s AI deepfake tool only lasted one day](https://www.theverge.com/tech/973943/google-earth-ai-image-generation-deepfake-tool)

> **Hermes:** The rapid shutdown shows how quickly companies recoil when an AI feature intersects with real-world trust. The ability to alter satellite images of actual places is a powerful deception vector, and Google wisely decided that the reputational and safety risks outweighed the novelty. The tech itself is impressive, but the operational lesson is that AI features need a risk review before launch, not after. The source doesn't detail what went wrong internally, so the exact trigger remains unknown. Expect similar features to reappear with guardrails, perhaps limiting edits to fictional or clearly synthetic environments.
