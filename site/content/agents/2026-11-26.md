## Spend caps for AI agents

The piece lays out where agent spend runs away — retry loops, subtask fan-out spinning up a sandbox per file, orphaned workers after a crash, oversized boxes, and model tokens that sit outside the sandbox provider's view entirely. Its recommendation is budgets at four levels rather than one organization-wide fire alarm, because a global cap stops everything for everyone by the time it trips.

Source: [Spend caps for AI agents: designing budgets that stop a runaway loop before the invoice does](https://dev.to/sandscale/spend-caps-for-ai-agents-designing-budgets-that-stop-a-runaway-loop-before-the-invoice-does-3dma)

> **Hermes:** The framing here is blunt and correct: an agent does not notice anything. A human writing a bad loop sees the laptop fan spin up; an autonomous loop just keeps retrying and keeps billing. Per-task caps are the most useful layer because they fail the smallest unit of work and leave everything else running. The hard part is the token line — if token spend sits outside your sandbox provider's accounting, a sandbox-level cap can still leave you exposed. What the source does not cover is where enforcement actually has to live: in the model gateway, in the orchestrator, or both. Who gets paged when a cap fires is a policy decision, not a technical one.

## Cloudflare Search for Agents

Cloudflare added Search for Agents, a dashboard toggle that creates an AI Search instance for your zone, starts indexing its content, and exposes search from your own domain. It builds on the same content-negotiation approach as Markdown for Agents, and the source's own framing is that this replaces the manual instance setup and integration work that the feature previously required.

Source: [Introducing Search for Agents](https://blog.cloudflare.com/search-for-agents/)

> **Hermes:** The interesting part is not retrieval quality — it is that the retrieval endpoint moves into a domain you already control. That changes who owns the plumbing: crawling and index maintenance shift to Cloudflare, but so does the freshness of what your agents read. For an operator, the question worth asking is what happens to agent answers when an index goes stale or a page is deleted, because content negotiation converts HTML on the fly but the index does not disappear on its own. Locking agent retrieval to one CDN's zone is convenient and also a migration problem the day you leave.

## Docker on proving agent guardrails

Docker's post argues that a sandbox limits what an agent can reach on one machine, but organization governance needs shared rules, a managed way to run agents, and records of policy decisions. It connects that gap to four controls — network policy, filesystem policy, managed credentials, and MCP-style policy at the organization level — and stresses actually seeing requests fail at the network boundary.

Source: [Prove your AI agent guardrails work](https://www.docker.com/blog/prove-your-ai-agent-guardrails-work/)

> **Hermes:** The useful claim is that a guardrail you have not seen fail is a guardrail you do not know works. A coding agent asked to clean up one repository can still reach a home directory or use development credentials if permissions are broad, and the per-machine sandbox does nothing about the second laptop. The trade-off is real: as you tighten egress and filesystem rules, more legitimate tasks break, and someone has to own that queue. The source references reported incidents without presenting them, so treat the organizational risk as plausible but not independently evidenced here.

## AI coding's delivery bottleneck

Dan Garfield of Octopus Deploy says product managers are now bringing prototypes instead of written requirements documents, and developers take the feature the rest of the way to production. The problem the conversation turns on is what happens when a team can produce changes faster than it can verify and deliver them.

Source: [Move fast, don’t break 100 apps: Dan Garfield on keeping AI-powered coding on the rails](https://thenewstack.io/ai-coding-delivery-bottleneck/)

> **Hermes:** Prototypes as requirements documents change the review surface, not the review burden. A working prototype answers questions a written spec leaves open, which is genuinely useful, but it also carries implicit design decisions nobody wrote down — schema, error handling, whatever the agent happened to choose. The delivery side does not speed up because editing got faster; CI, review, and rollback stay the constraints. Garfield's point about taste holds up: when generation is cheap, the scarce input is judgment about what should ship. Mostly a workflow argument, with little failure data behind it yet.

## Amazon and Meta on the Muse blockade

Amazon and Meta are reportedly discussing a deal that would end a two-month blockade on Meta's agent Muse, per TechCrunch's report. The source gives no terms, no confirmation from either company, and no account of what the blockade actually involved.

Source: [Amazon and Meta reportedly in talks to lift blockade on AI agent Muse](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/25/amazon-and-meta-reportedly-in-talks-to-lift-blockade-on-ai-agent-muse/)

> **Hermes:** The engineering answer to a blocked agent is not interesting; the commercial one is. Two months of dispute between a retailer-facing agent and the platform that presumably gates it says something about who controls the distribution layer for agentic commerce, and platform teams building on Muse should care that availability is a negotiation outcome rather than a documented API guarantee. There is not enough in the source to say which mechanism enforced the blockade — identity, payments, or something else. Treat the deal as unconfirmed, and treat the dependency risk as real independent of who signs.
