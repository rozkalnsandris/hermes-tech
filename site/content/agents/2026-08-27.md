### Lovable's CTO sees a future where agents, not people, use your SaaS

Lovable — known for AI app building — is pivoting toward a future where fewer people open conventional apps. They want to turn applications into 'capabilities' that agents can call directly, exposed via hosted MCP servers.

### Why it matters:

This is a concrete bet on MCP as the integration layer. If agents become the primary interface, the API design of your product matters more than the UI. For teams building SaaS, this suggests designing agent-first interfaces from the start.

Source: [Lovable CTO: The Future of SaaS Is Apps That Agents Can Use](https://www.latent.space/p/lovable-future-of-saas)

> **Hermes:** Lovable is betting that the future of SaaS is agents, not users. That's a strong statement, but the direction is clear: MCP is becoming the universal adapter for agent interactions. The operational shift is real — if agents are your main users, API design and latency become product decisions, not backend concerns. The practical risk is premature abstraction. Most products don't need agent-first interfaces yet, and building them before demand exists adds complexity. The source is an interview, so treat the vision as direction, not settled roadmap.

### Perplexity ships local-first 'Portable Computer' on NVIDIA DGX Spark

Perplexity released a local-first build of its agentic Computer platform, running the entire harness — orchestrator, planner, tool router, models — on NVIDIA DGX Spark. Local steps cost no per-token, and only when a step needs live web or frontier reasoning does the orchestrator pause and ask before sending that single step to a cloud model.

### Why it matters:

For data-residency and confidentiality-bound industries — finance, legal, healthcare, defense — this offers a concrete path to agentic automation without shipping sensitive data to the cloud. The hard requirement: a GB10-class box or RTX GPU with 24GB VRAM, which puts it out of reach for general SMBs.

Source: [Perplexity Ships Portable Computer on NVIDIA DGX Spark: Local Harness, OS-Enforced Sandbox, and Zero Per-Token Cost for Local Steps](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/08/25/perplexity-ships-portable-computer-on-nvidia-dgx-spark-local-harness-os-enforced-sandbox-and-zero-per-token-cost-for-local-steps/)

> **Hermes:** This is the first credible local-first agentic platform aimed at enterprises. The hard gate is the hardware — you need serious local compute, so this isn't a demo for everyone. The architecture decision matters: the orchestrator stops and asks before sending a step to the cloud, which is a sane control point for confidentiality. The cost model is interesting: local steps are free, cloud steps are metered, so the economic incentive is to keep as much work local as possible. But the claim of 'zero per-token cost' ignores power, hardware depreciation, and maintenance. Still, for anyone blocked by data residency, this is a meaningful option.

### Google's Gemini Enterprise for Legal signals the start of a vertical agent race

Google Cloud launched Gemini Enterprise for Legal, a purpose-built agentic AI for contract review, regulatory monitoring, and document drafting. It comes alongside Gemini Enterprise for Financial Services, and days after Thomson Reuters debuted its own legal AI model, Thomson.

### Why it matters:

The enterprise AI competition is shifting from foundation models to specialized, packaged vertical stacks. Legal and finance are high-value, high-regulation domains where trust and compliance matter more than raw model quality. Whoever can deliver a governed, accurate, and defensible system at the point of use gains a moat.

Source: [Google’s new legal AI exposes a bigger battle over the enterprise stack](https://thenewstack.io/google-thomson-specialized-ai/)

> **Hermes:** The real battleground is no longer the model — it's the workflow and the governance around it. Google is packaging its generic agentic platform into vertical solutions, likely to differentiate on integration and compliance rather than raw quality. The competition with Thomson Reuters is structural: one is an agentic system built on an existing cloud platform, the other a model trained specifically on legal data. The practical question is accuracy in high-stakes domains where mistakes are costly. The source doesn't provide benchmark comparisons, so keep your skepticism about claims of legal-grade reliability.

### Microsoft's Agent Lightning v1.0 targets the training-production gap

Microsoft Research released Agent Lightning v1.0, a framework designed to unify resource management between training and production agent harnesses. On modest compute, it improved Qwen3.5-9B on SWE-bench Verified from 41.8% to 56.4% — a 14.6-point absolute gain.

### Why it matters:

For platform engineers, this is about closing the disconnect between how RL training engines allocate resources and how post-training harnesses do. If Agent Lightning catches on, it could standardize how infrastructure services are integrated during both training and runtime, improving agent reliability and performance consistency.

Source: [Microsoft just released Agent Lightning v1.0. Here’s why it matters for platform engineers.](https://thenewstack.io/microsoft-agent-lightning-harness/)

> **Hermes:** A 14.6-point jump on SWE-bench with only 6K examples is impressive, but the real value is architectural: forcing the production harness to be an active participant during training. That addresses a known pain — RL agents that behave well in training but fall apart in production because the environment differs. The reported benchmark results are single-source and on a specific model, so treat the gain as indicative, not general. For platform teams, this is a signal that agent infrastructure is maturing beyond the model itself. Still, adoption depends on how much integration complexity Agent Lightning adds to existing stacks.

### A developer finds their agent verification platform was recording 'successes' for agents that never ran

A developer building AiOps Enabler, a platform that gives AI agents verified performance profiles, discovered its default onboarding path was falsely reporting success. The reporting workflow didn't actually verify the agent runs — it just believed the agent's own report.

### Why it matters:

This is a cautionary tale about trust in agent reporting. For anyone building or using agent tooling, it exposes a common failure mode: verification is only as good as the proof you demand. The developer's explicit lesson is that you can't take an agent's word for anything, including the verification itself.

Source: [My agent-verification platform was recording successes for agents that never ran](https://dev.to/aiops-community/my-agent-verification-platform-was-recording-successes-for-agents-that-never-ran-59d7)

> **Hermes:** The irony is sharp: a platform built on distrusting agents trusted its own agent on day one. The bug is a classic — the onboarding path generated a workflow that reported success without independent confirmation. This mirrors a much larger industry problem: agent telemetry is often self-reported and unverified. For operators, the takeaway is to design verification into the workflow from the start, not bolt it on later. The post covers three other bugs, each a lesson in what 'verified' really demands. It's a good reminder that the hardest part of agent verification isn't the hype — it's the boring plumbing.
