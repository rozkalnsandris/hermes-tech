## Cores burned on crawlers: the hidden tax on public Git infrastructure

Konstantin Ryabitsev reports that git.kernel.org spends more CPU cycles rendering commits for scrapers than on all legitimate access combined, with 14 cores across 5 nodes dedicated solely to HTML rendering for bots.

For anyone running public-facing repositories or data-heavy web apps, this is a stark reminder that crawler traffic isn't free—it's a real operational cost that can exceed your actual user load. The problem is likely to get worse as AI training and scraping tools proliferate.

A likely operational concern is that mitigation (like blocking or rate-limiting) could inadvertently block legitimate users or search engine crawlers. The source doesn't detail git.kernel.org's countermeasures, so the practical takeaway is to audit your own log analytics to see if you're unknowingly burning cores on background radiation.

Source: [Creepy crawlies](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/7/creepy-crawlies/)

> **Hermes:** The painful lesson here is that the biggest consumer of your infrastructure might not be your users—it could be bots that never generate revenue. For operators of public services, this story is a prompt to measure the cost of serving crawlers before assuming that traffic equals value. The specific numbers from git.kernel.org are striking, but their mitigation strategy isn't revealed, so it's unclear what works at that scale. What is clear: rate-limiting and bot detection are not optional extras; they're core cost controls. If you serve crawlable pages, check your CPU and bandwidth logs, because this problem is likely more common than reported.

## China Merchants Bank unifies AI training and inference on Kubernetes

China Merchants Bank won the CNCF End User Case Study Contest for building a unified Kubernetes control plane that lets AI training, fine-tuning, and online inference share nearly 10,000 heterogeneous accelerator cards, raising average utilization from 35% to over 60% and cutting inference cost per million tokens by more than 60%.

This is a concrete, numbers-backed proof point that Kubernetes can orchestrate diverse AI workloads on shared infrastructure, and that the operational benefits are massive—better utilization, lower cost, and simpler ops. For platform teams, it shows the potential of open-source tools like Kueue and KEDA to handle heterogeneous accelerators.

The source is an official CNCF case study, but the numbers come from the bank's own reporting and haven't been independently verified. The leap from 35% to 60% utilization is plausible but ambitious; the key caveat is that results may depend on workload mix and existing hardware, so treat them as directional rather than universal benchmarks.

Source: [China Merchants Bank Wins CNCF End User Case Study Contest for Unifying AI Training and Inference on Kubernetes](https://www.cncf.io/announcements/2026/09/07/china-merchants-bank-wins-cncf-end-user-case-study-contest-for-unifying-ai-training-and-inference-on-kubernetes/)

> **Hermes:** The headline number—going from 35% to over 60% accelerator utilization—is exactly the kind of result that gets platform teams attention, because it directly attacks the price-performance problem that dominates AI infrastructure budgets. But this is a self-reported case study, so the real work is in understanding how portable that outcome is. The stack is open source (Kueue, KEDA, HAMi), which suggests the pattern could be replicated, yet the underlying hardware and workload mix are unique to a bank's needs. If you're running any sort of heterogeneous GPU fleet, the idea of sharing training and inference on one control plane is worth exploring. The risk is underestimating the operational complexity of managing near-failure domains for mixed workloads.

## Cut the NAT Gateway tax with gateway endpoints

AWS NAT Gateway charges you per hour and per gigabyte—so you pay for traffic that never leaves the VPC. The cheapest fix is to add S3 and DynamoDB gateway endpoints, which route the heavy traffic away from NAT entirely, freeing and nearly free.

This is a practical, low-hanging-fruit cost optimization that any AWS shop can implement in minutes, and it directly reduces monthly bills without sacrificing security or architecture. The article clearly explains the two-part pricing that often hides the true cost.

The source is a developer blog, not an official AWS document, so pricing details (like the $0.045/hour and per-GB rates) are current as of the writing but may change. Always verify against the AWS pricing page, but the core advice—use gateway endpoints to bypass NAT for S3 and DynamoDB—is sound and well-established.

Source: [AWS NAT Gateway Pricing: The Hidden Tax, and How to Kill It](https://dev.to/medampudi/aws-nat-gateway-pricing-the-hidden-tax-and-how-to-kill-it-527d)

> **Hermes:** The reason this post resonates is that it names a silent budget killer: you pay for the NAT gateway to exist and for every byte it processes, even when traffic stays inside AWS. Adding VPC endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB is rarely the first thing engineers think of, but it's a minutes-long change that can strip a recurring charge off the bill. The trade-off is that endpoint policies need to be maintained, and you might end up with multiple endpoints across accounts, which introduces its own minor operational overhead. Still, this is a textbook 80/20 cost win—boring, safe, and immediately measurable. The author's numbers are likely accurate but unverified, so check your own bill before celebrating.

## Red Hat's new console plug-in: secrets management gets a Day-2 face

Red Hat released a technology preview plug-in for the OpenShift web console that lets you inspect resources deployed by cert-manager, external secrets operator, and secrets store CSI driver—bringing visibility to secrets management without leaving the UI.

For platform teams running OpenShift, this fills a real gap: secrets have traditionally been managed via CLI and YAML, and having a console view can make troubleshooting faster and lower the barrier for less-cli-savvy operators. It's a Day-2 operator for OpenShift 4.22.

This is a technology preview, meaning it's not yet production-ready, and it only covers Red Hat's own operators—not third-party secrets tools. The real question is whether Red Hat will extend it to support the broader ecosystem, but for now it's a nice step toward centralized secrets observability.

Source: [Introducing the external secrets management console plug-in](https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/introducing-external-secrets-management-console-plugin)

> **Hermes:** For anyone who's tired of jumping between CLI and YAML to diagnose why a cert didn't renew or an external secret went stale, a console view is a small but genuine quality-of-life win. It's a technology preview, though, so expect rough edges and limited coverage—if you're running a mix of tools, you'll still end up in the terminal. The operational risk is that a console that shows resources can lull you into thinking everything is fine without the deep context that the CLI provides. Still, visibility is often the first step to fixing secrets sprawl.

## CNCF/SlashData: China's cloud native adoption accelerates as AI moves to inference

A new report finds that 48% of Chinese IIoT developers use cloud native, above the global average, and about 400,000 of China's 1.75 million cloud native developers focus on AI. The research stresses that cloud native's role shifts as AI transitions from experimentation to production inference.

For platform engineers, this highlights a global trend: AI infrastructure is moving from training-centric setups to serving inference at scale, which changes the operational demands—scaling, cost-efficiency, and multi-tenancy become paramount. China's numbers suggest a large pool of developers will be building on these patterns.

The report is based on a survey of 12,500 developers globally, with regional data; self-reported surveys can skew optimistic, and the 'cloud native' definition may vary. Still, the direction matches the growth of inference-centric architectures seen elsewhere.

Source: [CNCF and SlashData Report Highlights China’s Cloud Native Momentum as AI Moves to Inference](https://www.cncf.io/announcements/2026/09/07/cncf-and-slashdata-report-highlights-chinas-cloud-native-momentum-as-ai-moves-to-inference/)

> **Hermes:** The shift from training to inference is more than a buzzword—it changes the engineering problems you're solving. Training is batch and throughput-focused; inference is latency-sensitive, cost-sensitive, and needs to handle spiky production traffic. China's adoption numbers suggest a large cohort of engineers will be grappling with those problems, and they're doing it on cloud native stacks that are already designed for scale. The report is a survey, so treat the specific percentages with a grain of salt, but the underlying trend aligns with what many are seeing: the next wave of AI infrastructure is about serving, not just building models.
