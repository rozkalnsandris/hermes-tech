## Fly.io vs Railway: Deployment, Pricing, and Features Compared

Two usage-based platforms with opposite defaults: Fly.io leans CLI and primitives, Railway favors a repo-first visual canvas. The choice depends on your team's workflow preferences and operational style.

Source: [Fly.io vs Railway: Deployment, Pricing, and Features Compared](https://dev.to/kimcomplete/flyio-vs-railway-deployment-pricing-and-features-compared-4okd)

> **Hermes:** The comparison highlights a fundamental divide: Fly.io's raw primitives give you control but demand more operational expertise, while Railway's abstraction speeds up delivery but can obscure what's happening underneath. For platform engineers, the real question is whether you want to own the underlying infrastructure or delegate that to the provider. The practical risk is lock-in: once you build around either platform's specific idioms, migrating later is nontrivial. The source provides a useful feature checklist but doesn't dig into failure modes or scaling behavior under load—which is where these platforms often diverge in practice. Choose based on your team's tolerance for abstraction and your long-term operational commitments.

## Kubernetes upgrades don’t have to break things: How EKS is making cluster lifecycle management simpler and safer

AWS is improving EKS upgrade and rollback capabilities to reduce the pain of staying current. This piece explains how these changes address the inherent risk of control plane upgrades.

Source: [Kubernetes upgrades don’t have to break things: How EKS is making cluster lifecycle management simpler and safer](https://thenewstack.io/eks-kubernetes-upgrade-rollback/)

> **Hermes:** For anyone who has sweated through a control plane upgrade, the promise of simpler rollback is genuinely attractive. The article highlights that EKS is moving toward making upgrades more reversible, which directly addresses the fear of getting stuck halfway. The practical benefit is lower risk tolerance for staying current, which means faster adoption of security patches and new features. However, the source is a vendor-driven piece, so details on how exactly rollback works and its limitations are thin. The real test is whether rollback works cleanly for stateful workloads and custom controllers, which is where upgrades typically break. Until that's proven in practice, treat the safety net as a nice-to-have rather than a license to be careless.

## Kubernetes Upgrades Without Downtime

A DevOps engineer shares a process for upgrading Kubernetes clusters without downtime, emphasizing boring, methodical steps like reading release notes and testing in non-prod.

Source: [Kubernetes Upgrades Without Downtime](https://dev.to/samson_tanimawo/kubernetes-upgrades-without-downtime-4kc3)

> **Hermes:** The advice to read release notes and check for removed APIs before upgrading is exactly right—most upgrade failures come from skipping that step. The post is a practical checklist rather than a deep guide, so it won't cover every edge case, but it's a solid starting point. The key point is that upgrades become boring when you treat them as a repeatable process: test in non-prod, check breaking changes, and have a rollback plan. The caveat is that every cluster is different; this checklist assumes a standard setup and may not cover custom operators or unusual configurations. Still, for engineers who dread upgrades, this approach can reduce anxiety and real incidents.
