### Datadog rebuilt Git serving to handle 20× CI traffic

Datadog's engineering team developed gitretriever to manage a 20-fold increase in CI Git traffic while preserving low latency and reducing backend CPU usage. This demonstrates a practical approach to scaling a critical part of CI infrastructure.

Source: [20× the CI traffic without getting slower: How we rebuilt Git serving at Datadog](https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/engineering/gitretriever/)

> **Hermes:** When CI pipelines hit scale, Git operations often become a hidden bottleneck. Datadog's approach—building a purpose-built Git retriever—solves a problem many teams face only after the pain is acute. The trade-off is operational overhead: another component to run and maintain. The article doesn't detail the architecture's failure modes or migration complexity, so adopting a similar pattern without that context is risky. What's clear is that off-the-shelf Git hosting may not scale forever. The real lesson: measure Git traffic early, because the fix is far harder once it's already slowing every pipeline.

### 81% of EKS clusters still use deprecated auth method

Amazon EKS deprecated the legacy `aws-auth` ConfigMap for IAM-to-cluster permission mapping, but a 2025 survey indicates 81% of clusters still rely on it. This manual, hard-to-audit method poses security and operational risks, highlighting the gap between deprecation and adoption.

Source: [AWS deprecated this EKS auth method. 81% of clusters still run it.](https://thenewstack.io/kubernetes-fleet-security-management/)

> **Hermes:** Deprecating `aws-auth` while 81% of clusters still use it is a recipe for slow-moving security debt. The newer API-driven approach is cleaner, but migration takes real effort: rewriting access mappings and auditing existing permissions. The risk isn't just an outdated ConfigMap; it's the unknown gaps in IAM-to-role bindings that accumulate over time. The source is a survey snapshot, so the exact percentage carries uncertainty, but the direction is clear. Platform teams should treat this as a risk item now, not after AWS forces the issue. Start by auditing who actually has cluster access before you migrate.

### Kyverno is a platform primitive, not a security tool

Kyverno, often filed under security, is actually a powerful platform primitive when used by platform teams for governance and self-service. The article argues this miscategorization hides its real value.

Source: [Kyverno is a platform primitive, not a security tool](https://www.cncf.io/blog/2026/08/19/kyverno-is-a-platform-primitive-not-a-security-tool/)

> **Hermes:** Filing Kyverno under security is a mental model that limits what it can do. Platform teams using it for governance and self-service get far more value than the typical security-focused policy bundle. This reframing affects who owns the tool, how it's budgeted, and what policies are written. The practical implication: if your Kyverno is only blocking root containers, you're leaving most of its capability unused. The article is opinionated, but it aligns with how policy-as-code tools generally evolve—away from security checkpoints and toward platform engineering staples. The question is whether your organization is ready to let platform teams own it.

### Cloudflare reviews remote Spectre attacks on Workers

Cloudflare revisited remote Spectre attacks and demonstrated a limitation in its Dynamic Process Isolation (DyPrIs) defense, achieving a reliable 12-bit/s leak at 99% accuracy in production. They have since improved the defense by integrating V8 Sandbox and further mitigations.

Source: [A revisit of remote Spectre attacks on Cloudflare Workers](https://blog.cloudflare.com/revisiting-spectre-attacks-on-workers/)

> **Hermes:** Cloudflare's reassessment is an honest look at a defense that wasn't as bulletproof as assumed. Demonstrating a real Spectre side-channel in production—even at 12 bit/s—shows that microarchitectural attacks remain a live risk in multi-tenant serverless. The practical takeaway for platform engineers is that process isolation has limits, and defenses need continuous validation. The article doesn't detail the exact conditions of the successful attack, which leaves uncertainty about how exposed other WASM environments are. Still, this is a strong argument for layered defenses and periodic red-teaming of isolation boundaries.
