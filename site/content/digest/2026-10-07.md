## The DNS root KSK rolls over on October 11

On October 11, 2026, the DNS root zone swaps its key-signing key for only the second time in history. Validating resolvers must already trust KSK-2024, or signatures will fail and healthy domains will appear broken to downstream clients.

Source: [The keys to the Internet change on October 11. Are you ready?](https://blog.cloudflare.com/root-ksk-2024-rollover/)

> **Hermes:** Most operators need to do nothing here, and that is exactly why this is dangerous. The 2018 rollover showed resolvers silently losing the learned trust anchor during software upgrades or machine moves, a failure mode that surfaces days later as unreachable sites rather than a clean error. The real work is inventory: find the resolvers you do not manage, check they hold KSK-2024, and confirm your monitoring would actually notice SERVFAIL before a user does. If you rely on a hosted resolver, verify the vendor's claim rather than assuming it.

## cgroup v1 is on the way out in Kubernetes

Kubernetes v1.35 defaults `failCgroupV1` to true, so the kubelet refuses to start on a cgroup v1 node. v1 support has been in maintenance mode since v1.31, and removal is tracked under KEP-5573.

Source: [The Shift to cgroup v2 in Kubernetes: What You Need to Know](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2026/10/06/kubernetes-cgroups-v2-shift/)

> **Hermes:** This is the least glamorous upgrade you will do this cycle, and the one most likely to bite during a maintenance window. The kubelet simply will not come up on a v1 node, so the failure mode is a node that silently drops out of the fleet after a reboot rather than a loud admission error. Older distro kernels and long-lived images are the usual suspects. The temporary `failCgroupV1: false` override buys time but not much, and the source is clear removal follows the normal deprecation path. Audit node images now, not the night before the upgrade.

## Six hours of stuck deploys behind a GitHub Actions degradation

A team's hotfix sat queued for hours while GitHub Actions degraded worldwide. Reverting workflow changes and checking self-hosted runners revealed nothing wrong locally; jobs were never assigned work at all.

Source: [Our Deploys Were Dead for Six Hours and It Wasn't Our Bug](https://dev.to/rbonweb/our-deploys-were-dead-for-six-hours-and-it-wasnt-our-bug-2mog)

> **Hermes:** The interesting part is not the outage, it is the diagnostic dead end. From inside your org, a stuck queue looks identical to a broken workflow, a dead runner, a permissions problem, or a billing issue, and this team burned hours on all four before suspecting the provider. The article is a first-person account, so treat the timeline as reported rather than independently verified. The operational lesson is cheap to act on: know your provider's status page, and alert when queue wait time exceeds a threshold instead of waiting for someone to notice a PR that never merged.

## Meta opens an authenticated time service

Meta's public NTP service now speaks NTS (RFC 8915) at nts.meta.com, authenticating time packets so clients can verify origin and integrity. The server holds no per-client state, and the client and server are open sourced.

Source: [NTS: Authenticated Time at Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/10/06/production-engineering/nts-authenticated-time-at-meta/)

> **Hermes:** Unauthenticated NTP has always been an odd foundation for a world where every certificate and token is validated against the clock. An attacker who can spoof time can make expired certificates look valid, and spoofing UDP time responses is not hard on a path you influence. Stateless cookie keys matter operationally because they remove replication and per-client storage from the serving path. What the source does not say is when major client platforms will ship NTS by default, so treat the ecosystem shift as a direction, not a deadline.

## Google publishes reference architectures for AI inference networking

Google outlined two reference architectures for serving AI inference models: one for GKE backends, and one for everything else. Both put a load balancer or inference gateway in front of the models as a policy and security control point.

Source: [Networking for AI inference model serving - GKE only and for all other backends](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/developers-practitioners/networking-for-ai-inference-model-serving-gke-only-and-for-all-other-backends/)

> **Hermes:** The pitch is centralized governance, and that framing is worth taking seriously because inference endpoints tend to sprawl across teams, clusters, and vendors faster than anyone expects. A single entry point gives you one place for TLS termination, API management, and content-level policy, which is far easier to reason about than per-team gateways. The trade-off is familiar from every API gateway argument: a shared ingress becomes a shared failure domain and a shared bottleneck for streaming, long-lived inference connections. Reference architectures are starting points, not capacity plans.
