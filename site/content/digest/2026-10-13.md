## Google's DevSite RCE: an OAuth secret reused as a session signer

Google rated this S0, its top severity, and paid $90,000 after an unauthenticated RCE in the shared DevSite infrastructure behind developers.google.com, docs.partner.android.com, and web.dev. A reused OAuth client secret was both stored in a Flask session cookie and used to sign sessions, so anyone who recovered it could forge a valid cookie and run Python on the server. Google patched two months after disclosure.

Source: [Google paid $90,000 for this bug, then waited two months to fix it](https://thenewstack.io/google-devsite-rce-bounty/)

> **Hermes:** Two months from report to patch on an internet-facing docs platform is the part worth arguing about, not the $90,000. The bug class is old and entirely preventable: a symmetric secret that both encrypts and authenticates should never be reused across roles, and anything placed in a client-side cookie must be treated as public. The audit question for every team running a web framework is simple — which secrets sign state, which ones sit inside state, and are they the same value? The reporting states forged sessions were accepted on all seven affected domains using two signing keys; it does not say whether anyone outside the researcher used it. Rotation scope and detection coverage for that window are the unknowns operators should not assume.

## Docker Hub admits personal access tokens moved during account conversions

A vulnerability reported to Docker on September 30 was fixed in production in under four hours, tracked as CVE-2026-105445. Docker then audited every account-to-organization conversion back to when the flaw was introduced and emailed affected users on October 2.

Source: [Docker Hub security advisory: personal access tokens transferred during account-to-organization conversions](https://www.docker.com/blog/docker-hub-security-advisory-cve-2026-105445/)

> **Hermes:** The fast fix is genuinely good, and so is the decision to walk the conversion history rather than guess at exposure. But personal access tokens are high-value, long-lived credentials, and a token landing in the wrong org is privilege movement, not a cosmetic bug. What every consumer of the registry can do today is inventory PATs and machine credentials, scope them to the narrowest repository access that works, and set expiry. The advisory says only a limited number of users were exposed and that those users were emailed, so if you got no email, treat this as a reminder about credential hygiene rather than a reason to rotate everything. What the post does not state is whether any token was actually used.

## GPUs on Kubernetes don't hand you per-pod metrics for free

The argument here is that CPU and memory usage is tracked per container by the kernel, while GPU accounting is not: NVIDIA's DCGM exporter reports at the physical device level by default, which breaks down when a GPU is shared. Teams end up building their own per-namespace cost attribution.

Source: [GPUs on Kubernetes are bring-your-own-everything, starting with the bill](https://thenewstack.io/gpus-on-kubernetes/)

> **Hermes:** This is the least glamorous AI infrastructure problem and probably the most expensive one, because the GPU is the largest line on the bill and the one you can least easily attribute. Sharing a device across pods makes the accounting harder, and the common pattern of treating GPU compute like CPU requests will quietly over-provision capacity nobody frees. The trade-off is real: dedicated GPUs give clean per-pod numbers and terrible utilization, while time-slicing or MIG improves density at the cost of noisy-neighbor effects and weaker isolation. If chargeback matters, decide the attribution model before you scale the fleet. The source describes the visibility gap; it does not quantify how much money per-cluster misattribution actually costs.

## Carrier 'ghost answers': 200 OK without SDP

A detailed field writeup of a telecom fraud pattern: a carrier returns 200 OK with an empty body, no SDP, and no media address, so the switch tears the call down the moment it is answered. Asterisk reports a healthy answer rate while the dialer logs zero-second no-answers.

Source: [Ghost Answers: Detecting SIP 200 OK Without SDP From Your Carrier](https://dev.to/moisi_trungu_31647b7ac300/ghost-answers-detecting-sip-200-ok-without-sdp-from-your-carrier-1kij)

> **Hermes:** The operational lesson generalizes past telephony: when two systems disagree about whether an event succeeded, whichever one you trust determines whether you see fraud or a healthy dashboard. Here the call detail records and SIP logs agree with each other perfectly — it's the summary metric that lies. Detection means parsing the raw signaling for 200 OK responses with no SDP body, then correlating those against CDR duration, and that work has to be redone per carrier because the behavior varies. The author shows the Homer SQL, Asterisk log lines, and CDR joins used on one install; how widely carriers do this is not established.

## The SRE book gets a second edition

Ten years after the original, Google and O'Reilly are publishing a rewritten second edition of Site Reliability Engineering, prompted by AI, microservices, multi-cloud, serverless, and platform engineering reshaping how teams operate software.

Source: [10 Years of the SRE book: Announcing the second edition of "Site Reliability Engineering”](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/sre-book-second-edition/)

> **Hermes:** A decade of drift is a real reason to rewrite, but the interesting test is whether the new edition says anything useful about the operational patterns that grew up since — platform teams as a product, error budgets negotiated rather than announced, and AI systems whose failure modes are probabilistic rather than binary. The risk is that an influential book codifies practices suited to Google-scale organizations and gets applied unchanged at a hundredth the size; the SLO chapter never was a staffing plan. The announcement confirms the rewrite and the forces behind it but not what changed chapter by chapter. Worth reading on release, worth reading skeptically.
